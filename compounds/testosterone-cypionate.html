What is testosterone cypionate?

Testosterone cypionate is testosterone — the body's primary male sex hormone — attached to a cypionate ester and suspended in oil for injection. Testosterone itself is identical to what the body makes; the ester is a chemical "time-release" handle that slows how quickly the hormone is released after injection, turning a molecule that would otherwise clear in hours into one that lasts for days.

In the United States it is one of the two most common injectable esters used for testosterone replacement therapy (TRT), alongside testosterone enanthate, which behaves very similarly. It is an FDA-approved medicine and a controlled substance, prescribed for men with hypogonadism — clinically low testosterone confirmed by symptoms and bloodwork.

Unlike most of the compounds catalogued here, testosterone cypionate is a well-established, approved drug with decades of clinical use. The relevant questions are therefore less "does it work" and more about correct diagnosis, dosing to a target, and ongoing monitoring — all of which belong with a prescribing clinician.

How testosterone cypionate is thought to work

Once injected, the oil depot releases testosterone gradually, and the ester is cleaved to free active testosterone that acts throughout the body:

  • Androgen receptor activation — testosterone binds androgen receptors in muscle, bone, skin, and other tissues, driving its masculinizing and anabolic effects.
  • Restoring physiological levels — in men with low testosterone, replacement aims to bring blood levels back into a normal range, which is associated with improvements in energy, libido, mood, and body composition.
  • Conversion to other hormones — some testosterone is converted to estradiol (via aromatase) and to DHT, and both of those downstream hormones have their own roles and side-effect profiles.
  • Feedback suppression — external testosterone signals the brain to dial back its own production, which is why natural testosterone and fertility typically decline on therapy.

The long ester is what makes the levels relatively steady between injections, and why splitting the weekly dose can smooth out peaks and troughs.

What the research shows

Testosterone replacement in men with diagnosed hypogonadism is well supported by clinical evidence and long clinical experience: it reliably raises testosterone into the normal range and improves symptoms such as low libido and reduced energy in appropriately selected patients. Guidelines from endocrinology bodies describe who should and should not be treated, and emphasize confirming low levels before starting.

Where the picture is more nuanced is in men without a clear deficiency, where benefits are smaller and less certain, and in long-term questions around cardiovascular and prostate outcomes, which continue to be studied. Non-medical use for physique or performance at supraphysiologic doses is a different situation entirely and carries greater risk. This page describes established medical use and is not a substitute for your prescriber's judgment.

How testosterone cypionate is commonly used

For replacement therapy, commonly referenced doses fall around 100-200 mg per week. Many modern protocols split that into smaller twice-weekly injections to keep levels steadier and reduce swings in mood and side effects. Historically it was given as a larger dose every two weeks, but more frequent, smaller dosing has become common. It is injected intramuscularly or, increasingly, subcutaneously.

Importantly, testosterone cypionate does not need to be reconstituted — unlike peptides supplied as a freeze-dried powder, it comes as a ready-to-use oil at a known concentration (for example 200 mg/mL). Commonly used with

On TRT, testosterone cypionate is sometimes paired with HCG to help preserve testicular size and fertility, and some protocols add an aromatase inhibitor when estrogen runs high. Related esters like testosterone enanthate behave almost identically. These are individualized clinical decisions guided by bloodwork — not generic add-ons.

Side effects & safety considerations

Possible effects include acne and oily skin, elevated red blood cell count (hematocrit), estrogen-related effects such as water retention or breast tissue sensitivity, testicular shrinkage and reduced fertility from suppressed natural production, and injection-site reactions. Because hematocrit can rise, periodic blood donation or dose adjustment is sometimes needed, and regular bloodwork — testosterone, estradiol, hematocrit, PSA where appropriate — is a standard part of therapy.

Testosterone is a controlled substance and is not appropriate for everyone; it is generally avoided in men with certain cancers and used cautiously in those with cardiovascular risk or untreated sleep apnea, and it should not be used by women who are or may become pregnant. None of this is medical advice. Testosterone cypionate should be prescribed, dosed, and monitored by a qualified healthcare provider.

Testosterone Cypionate FAQ

What is a common testosterone cypionate dose?

For testosterone replacement, commonly referenced doses fall around 100-200 mg per week, sometimes split into smaller twice-weekly injections to steady levels. Actual dosing is individualized to bloodwork and symptoms and must be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is testosterone cypionate FDA approved?

Yes. Testosterone cypionate is an FDA-approved injectable medicine for testosterone replacement in men with hypogonadism (clinically low testosterone). It is a controlled substance and is intended to be prescribed and monitored by a clinician.

What is the half-life of testosterone cypionate?

The cypionate ester gives it a long half-life of roughly 8 days, which is why weekly or twice-weekly injection schedules are typical. The long ester releases testosterone slowly from the injection site over time.

Does testosterone cypionate need to be reconstituted?

No. Unlike peptides supplied as a lyophilized powder, testosterone cypionate comes as a ready-to-use oil solution at a known concentration (such as 200 mg/mL). You do not mix it — but StackMindr's calculator still helps convert your target milligram dose into the exact volume or units to draw.

What are the side effects of testosterone cypionate?

Possible effects include acne, oily skin, elevated red blood cell count (hematocrit), estrogen-related effects such as water retention, testicular shrinkage and reduced fertility from suppressed natural production, and injection-site reactions. Regular bloodwork is standard. Discuss risks with your provider.

What is testosterone cypionate commonly used with on TRT?

On TRT it is sometimes paired with HCG to help preserve testicular function and fertility, and in some protocols an aromatase inhibitor is used if estrogen runs high. These are individualized clinical decisions, not generic add-ons, and should be guided by a provider and bloodwork.
