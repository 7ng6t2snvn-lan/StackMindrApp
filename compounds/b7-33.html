What is B7-33?

B7-33 is a single-chain analog of relaxin, a naturally occurring hormone best known for its role in pregnancy, where it remodels tissue and relaxes blood vessels. Relaxin itself is also a potent anti-fibrotic signal — it counteracts the tissue scarring that damages organs like the heart and kidneys over time.

The problem with relaxin as a therapeutic is that it is a complex two-chain hormone that is difficult and expensive to manufacture. B7-33 was engineered as a stripped-down, single-chain version derived from relaxin’s B-chain that retains the key anti-fibrotic signaling while being much simpler to produce. It is very much an early research compound — the data is preclinical, and it has essentially no established human track record.

How B7-33 is thought to work

What makes B7-33 scientifically interesting is not just that it mimics relaxin, but how it signals:

  • RXFP1 activation — it binds relaxin’s main receptor, RXFP1, the entry point for relaxin’s tissue-remodeling effects.
  • Biased signaling — it is reported to preferentially trigger the anti-fibrotic ERK1/2 pathway rather than the cAMP pathway, which researchers hope keeps the useful anti-scarring effect while limiting other actions.
  • Anti-fibrotic action — in animal models it reduced fibrosis (scar tissue) in the heart, kidney and lung.
  • Vascular healing — consistent with relaxin’s biology, it has shown effects supporting blood-vessel function and repair.

That “biased” signaling is the headline idea: a small, manufacturable peptide that captures relaxin’s most therapeutically valuable effect.

What the research shows

The evidence for B7-33 is preclinical. In animal models it has reduced fibrosis across several organ systems — cardiac, renal and pulmonary — and shown vascular-protective effects, which is genuinely promising given how damaging and hard-to-treat organ fibrosis is.

It is worth noting the wider relaxin context: the full hormone has itself been taken into large human trials in a pharmaceutical form (serelaxin) for acute heart failure, where results were ultimately disappointing — a reminder that relaxin-pathway biology has not translated into an approved therapy even in its most-studied form. B7-33 is a far earlier-stage, simplified relative of that effort.

But the honesty here matters: there are, as yet, no established human clinical trials demonstrating that B7-33 is safe or effective in people. It is one of the newer and less-studied peptides in circulation, and almost everything known about it comes from the laboratory. Anyone encountering strong claims about it should treat them as extrapolation from animal data, not proven human benefit. The honest summary is “promising mechanism, early animal data, unproven in humans.”

How B7-33 is commonly used

Because there is no clinical protocol, the figures that circulate — roughly 100-300 mcg daily by subcutaneous injection — are community-referenced values extrapolated from preclinical work, not validated dosing.

Injectable B7-33 arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Side effects & safety considerations

The defining safety issue for B7-33 is simply how little is known. With essentially no human data, there is no established side-effect profile, no safe-dose guidance, and no long-term safety record — a deeper level of uncertainty than for better-studied peptides.

As with any research-grade injectable, purity from suppliers is variable and unverifiable, and there is no medical oversight when it is self-administered. Because relaxin-pathway signaling affects blood vessels and tissue remodeling, effects could extend beyond the intended target in ways that have not been characterized in humans. This is not medical advice; given how early-stage it is, caution and a conversation with a qualified healthcare provider are especially warranted.

B7-33 FAQ

What is B7-33?

B7-33 is a single-chain analog of the hormone relaxin, engineered from relaxin’s B-chain to keep its anti-fibrotic signaling while being much simpler to manufacture than the full two-chain hormone.

How does B7-33 work?

It activates relaxin’s receptor RXFP1 with a reported bias toward the anti-fibrotic ERK1/2 pathway, reducing scar tissue (fibrosis) in the heart, kidney and lung and supporting vascular healing in animal models.

Is B7-33 proven in humans?

No. The evidence for B7-33 is preclinical — animal studies only. There are no established human clinical trials demonstrating that it is safe or effective in people.

Is B7-33 FDA approved?

No. B7-33 is an early research-stage peptide and is not approved to treat any condition.

What is B7-33 studied for?

Mainly organ fibrosis — reducing scar tissue in the heart, kidneys and lungs — and vascular healing, based on relaxin’s known anti-fibrotic biology. These findings come from animal research.

What is B7-33 commonly compared to?

It is discussed alongside Angiotensin (1-7), another anti-fibrotic, cardiovascular-protective peptide, and the mainstream recovery peptides BPC-157 and TB-500. These are educational comparisons, not a recommended protocol.
