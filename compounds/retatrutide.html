What is retatrutide?

Retatrutide (development code LY3437943) is an investigational metabolic drug from Eli Lilly. It belongs to the same incretin family as semaglutide and tirzepatide, but goes a step further: it is a triple agonist, engineered to activate three receptors at once — GLP-1, GIP, and the glucagon receptor.

The logic behind adding the glucagon arm is that glucagon, beyond its familiar role in raising blood sugar, is also involved in energy expenditure and fat metabolism. By combining glucagon activity with the appetite-suppressing effects of GLP-1 and GIP, retatrutide is designed to attack body weight from two directions at once — eating less while burning more.

The single most important thing to understand is its status: retatrutide is not an approved medicine. It is being studied in clinical trials, and while early results have drawn a lot of attention, its long-term safety and its final approved uses — if it is approved at all — are not yet settled. It is not a treatment for any condition today.

How retatrutide is thought to work

Retatrutide is designed to engage three hormone pathways simultaneously:

  • GLP-1 activation — slows gastric emptying, improves glucose-dependent insulin response, and reduces appetite through the brain.
  • GIP activation — a second incretin signal thought to enhance insulin response and influence fat handling.
  • Glucagon activation — the distinguishing feature; thought to raise energy expenditure and promote fat breakdown in the liver, adding a "burn more" effect on top of "eat less."
  • Combined metabolic effect — the three signals together are the rationale for the large weight changes reported in early trials.

Balancing glucagon's effects is also the central challenge: glucagon can raise blood glucose and heart rate, so the drug has to be tuned carefully, which is one reason trial participants are monitored closely.

What the research shows

Retatrutide's evidence base is early but promising. A phase 2 trial in people with obesity reported substantial average weight reductions over the study period, and separate early-phase work has examined its effects in type 2 diabetes and in metabolic liver disease. These findings generated significant interest because the average weight changes reported were large relative to earlier agents.

The honest framing is that these are early-stage results, not the basis of an approval. Larger and longer phase 3 trials are needed to confirm the benefits, define who it is appropriate for, and characterize long-term safety. This page describes an experimental compound; it does not establish efficacy or safety, and nothing here is a recommendation to use retatrutide outside a clinical trial.

How retatrutide is commonly used

Because retatrutide is investigational, there is no approved dosing regimen. In trials it is given as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection using slow dose escalation — starting low and stepping up over time, the same titration logic used across this drug class to limit nausea. Reported trial ranges have run from low starting doses up to roughly 12 mg weekly at the upper end.

Research-grade material sold as a lyophilized powder has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use, and the draw math depends on your vial size, water volume, and target dose. Commonly stacked with

Retatrutide should never be combined with other incretin drugs. It already acts on three receptors, so adding semaglutide or tirzepatide offers no benefit and sharply raises the risk of severe side effects. People researching it most often compare it with those two approved agents rather than stacking.

Side effects & safety considerations

In clinical trials the most common side effects were gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and constipation — generally worst during dose escalation. Because the glucagon pathway can raise heart rate and affect glucose handling, trial protocols monitor participants closely, and the drug's safety profile is still being characterized.

The overriding caution is that retatrutide is experimental. There is no established long-term human safety record, no approved product, and purity of research-grade material sold outside of trials is unverified. Nothing on this page is medical advice or an endorsement of use. Anyone considering retatrutide should understand it is unapproved and should discuss it with a qualified healthcare provider.

Retatrutide FAQ

What is a common retatrutide dose?

Retatrutide is investigational, so there is no approved dose. Trials have explored a range from low starting doses up to roughly 12 mg weekly, reached through slow titration. Commonly referenced figures are educational reference only and are not a recommendation; any use should be discussed with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is retatrutide FDA approved?

No. Retatrutide is an experimental drug still in clinical trials. It has not been approved by the FDA for any use, and long-term safety is not yet established. It is not a treatment for any condition.

How is retatrutide different from semaglutide and tirzepatide?

Semaglutide targets one receptor (GLP-1) and tirzepatide targets two (GLP-1 and GIP). Retatrutide is a triple agonist that targets three — GLP-1, GIP, and the glucagon receptor. The added glucagon activity is thought to raise energy expenditure on top of appetite suppression.

Can you stack retatrutide with other GLP-1 drugs?

No. Retatrutide should never be combined with semaglutide, tirzepatide, or other incretin agonists. Because it already acts on multiple receptors, adding another agent from the class offers no benefit and sharply increases the risk of severe side effects.

What are the reported side effects of retatrutide?

In trials the most common effects were gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, and constipation — typically worst during dose escalation. Because the glucagon pathway can raise heart rate and affect glucose, trial participants are monitored closely. Full long-term safety is still being studied.
