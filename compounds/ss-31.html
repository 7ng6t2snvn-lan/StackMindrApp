What is SS-31?

SS-31 — also known as elamipretide, MTP-131, or by its earlier research name Bendavia — is a synthetic tetrapeptide (just four amino acids) engineered to target the mitochondria, the energy-producing compartments inside your cells. It was developed by Stealth BioTherapeutics and has one of the deepest clinical research programs of any compound in this space.

What makes it unusual is where it acts. Most peptides discussed here signal a receptor on the cell surface. SS-31 instead travels into the cell and concentrates in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it binds a distinctive phospholipid called cardiolipin. That targeting is the whole point of the molecule — it was designed to go exactly there and nowhere else in particular.

It's worth being clear up front: despite years of trials, SS-31 is not an approved drug, and it is not established as a treatment for any condition.

How SS-31 is thought to work

SS-31's proposed benefits all trace back to one anchor point — cardiolipin — and what stabilizing it does downstream:

  • Cardiolipin binding — SS-31 selectively associates with cardiolipin, a phospholipid found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane and essential to that membrane's tightly folded "cristae" shape.
  • Protecting the electron transport chain — the respiratory complexes that generate ATP are organized along those cristae and anchored by cardiolipin. By stabilizing cardiolipin, SS-31 is thought to preserve that organization and support more efficient energy production.
  • Reducing oxidative stress — a well-ordered respiratory chain is proposed to "leak" fewer reactive oxygen species (ROS), lowering the oxidative damage that builds up when mitochondria are failing.
  • Preserving mitochondrial structure — in models of injury, disease, and aging, SS-31 is associated with maintaining cristae architecture and overall mitochondrial integrity.

The useful mental model: SS-31 is a mitochondrial membrane stabilizer, not a stimulant. It isn't trying to push the cell harder — it's trying to keep the energy machinery structurally intact so it runs cleanly.

What the research shows

SS-31/elamipretide is an outlier among the peptides in this library: it has an actual, substantial program of human clinical trials, not just animal data. It has been studied in primary mitochondrial myopathy (a genetic mitochondrial disease), Barth syndrome, heart failure and ischemia-reperfusion injury, and dry age-related macular degeneration (geographic atrophy).

The honest read on those results is mixed. A large trial in primary mitochondrial myopathy did not meet its primary endpoint; some other studies and open-label extensions reported signals worth following up, but nothing that has yet earned an approval. So while the mechanism is well-characterized and the evidence base is deep by peptide standards, SS-31 has not demonstrated clear, repeatable clinical benefit in the places it's been tested the hardest.

That matters for how it's used off-label. The popular use of SS-31 for general "mitochondrial support," fatigue, exercise recovery, or anti-aging is an extrapolation from the mechanism and from disease trials — not something those trials have demonstrated in healthy people.

How SS-31 is commonly used

In clinical trials, elamipretide was given as a once-daily subcutaneous injection, reconstituted from a lyophilized powder. Commonly referenced community protocols follow a similar pattern — daily or around five days a week, frequently run in blocks and then cycled off rather than taken continuously.

As an injectable, SS-31 arrives as a freeze-dried powder that is reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use, and the draw volume depends on your vial size and dilution. Commonly stacked with

SS-31 sits squarely in the mitochondrial and longevity toolbox, and it's usually discussed alongside other compounds that approach cellular energy from different angles. MOTS-c, a mitochondrial-derived peptide, and NAD+, the coenzyme at the center of energy metabolism, are the most common companions. Side effects & safety considerations

In clinical trials, elamipretide was generally described as well tolerated, with injection-site reactions (redness, irritation, itching) being the most commonly reported issue. Serious adverse effects were uncommon in the studied populations.

The bigger caveats are the familiar ones for an unapproved compound. Grey-market SS-31 isn't quality-controlled, so purity and actual content vary by supplier; there's no long-term safety record for the way it's used outside trials; and because it acts on fundamental cellular energy machinery, it isn't a casual DIY compound. None of this is medical advice — anyone considering SS-31 should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their situation.

SS-31 FAQ

What is SS-31 (elamipretide)?

SS-31 is a synthetic mitochondria-targeting tetrapeptide, also known as elamipretide, MTP-131, or Bendavia. Unlike most peptides it doesn't act on a cell-surface receptor — it travels into the cell and concentrates in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it binds a phospholipid called cardiolipin. It is one of the most extensively studied mitochondrial compounds and is not an approved drug.

How does SS-31 work?

SS-31 binds cardiolipin in the inner mitochondrial membrane. By stabilizing cardiolipin it is thought to preserve the folded cristae structure and the organization of the electron transport chain, supporting more efficient ATP production and reducing the leak of reactive oxygen species. It's best understood as a mitochondrial membrane stabilizer rather than a stimulant.

What is cardiolipin and why does it matter?

Cardiolipin is a phospholipid found almost exclusively in the inner mitochondrial membrane, where it is essential to the membrane's folded cristae shape and to anchoring the respiratory chain. Cardiolipin becomes damaged or depleted in mitochondrial dysfunction and aging, which is why a compound that binds and stabilizes it is of interest.

Is SS-31 FDA approved?

No. Despite an unusually deep clinical research program — including trials in primary mitochondrial myopathy, Barth syndrome, heart failure, and dry macular degeneration — SS-31/elamipretide is not approved for any indication, and results have been mixed. Its use for general mitochondrial support or anti-aging is extrapolation beyond what trials have shown.

How is SS-31 taken?

In clinical trials it was given as a once-daily subcutaneous injection, reconstituted from a lyophilized powder. Commonly referenced community protocols use it daily or about five days a week, often in cycles. This is educational reference only — any protocol should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

What is SS-31 commonly stacked with?

It sits in the mitochondrial and longevity toolbox and is most often discussed alongside MOTS-c (a mitochondrial-derived peptide) and NAD+, with some people also referencing Humanin — all approached as different angles on supporting mitochondrial function.
