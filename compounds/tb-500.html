What is TB-500?

TB-500 is a synthetic peptide that is closely related to thymosin beta-4 (often written Tβ4), a protein that occurs naturally throughout the body and is especially concentrated in blood platelets and at sites of tissue injury. Thymosin beta-4 is a 43-amino-acid protein involved in cell building and repair; TB-500 is a shorter, lab-made peptide designed around its most active region — the part responsible for binding actin, a key structural protein inside cells.

Because TB-500 reproduces that active segment, it is frequently discussed as a stand-in for the full protein, and the two names are often used interchangeably in peptide communities even though they are not chemically identical. What draws attention to TB-500 is its reputation for systemic rather than purely local action — it is described as circulating widely and reaching tissue throughout the body, which is part of why it is popularly associated with overall recovery and flexibility rather than one specific joint.

It is important to frame this accurately: the bulk of the evidence behind TB-500 and thymosin beta-4 comes from laboratory and animal research, including veterinary use in horses. It is not an approved human medicine and is not established as a treatment for any condition.

How TB-500 is thought to work

The mechanisms attributed to TB-500 in the research literature center on actin regulation and cell movement — the processes that let the body rebuild damaged tissue. The actions most often described in animal and laboratory studies include:

  • Actin regulation — binding G-actin and influencing the assembly of the cell's internal scaffolding, which is fundamental to how cells change shape and move.
  • Cell migration — promoting the movement of repair cells toward areas of injury, a step that matters for closing wounds and remodeling tissue.
  • Angiogenesis — supporting the formation of new blood vessels, which improves delivery of oxygen and nutrients to recovering tissue.
  • Modulating inflammation — described in experimental models as helping to temper the inflammatory response during healing.

Together these pathways are the reason TB-500 is so often discussed in the context of soft-tissue recovery and flexibility. The consistent caveat is that these mechanisms are characterized mostly in animals and cell models, and how cleanly they translate to humans is not well established.

What the research shows

Research on thymosin beta-4 and TB-500 has explored wound healing, muscle and tendon repair, cardiac tissue after injury, and corneal healing — largely in rodents and other animal models. Thymosin beta-4 itself has been investigated in some early human studies for specific indications such as wound and eye-surface healing, but TB-500 as sold and used in peptide communities has not been validated in large, high-quality human trials for the recovery uses it is popularly associated with.

The honest summary is "biologically interesting, under-studied in people." Much of what you will read about TB-500 for injuries is anecdotal. TB-500 is also on the World Anti-Doping Agency's prohibited list, so athletes in tested sport should treat it as banned. If you see a confident claim that TB-500 heals a specific injury on a specific timeline, treat it with skepticism.

How TB-500 is commonly used

Commonly referenced protocols use 2 to 2.5 mg, roughly twice weekly. Many of the protocols people describe follow a two-stage pattern: a higher-frequency "loading" block for the first several weeks, followed by a lower-frequency maintenance phase. It is administered by subcutaneous injection, and because its action is described as systemic, injection site is usually treated as a matter of convenience rather than something that needs to be near the target area.

TB-500 arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Commonly stacked with

TB-500 is most often paired with BPC-157. The two are frequently described as complementary for soft-tissue recovery — BPC-157 associated more with localized repair and angiogenesis, TB-500 with systemic cell migration and flexibility. People interested in TB-500 also tend to compare it with other thymosin beta-4-derived peptides.

Side effects & safety considerations

In anecdotal reports and the limited available research, TB-500 is generally described as well tolerated, with injection-site reactions (redness, mild irritation) and occasional transient fatigue or head-rush being the most commonly mentioned complaints. Because it is not a regulated medicine, product purity varies widely between suppliers, which is its own safety concern.

The central limitation is that there is no long-term human safety record to point to. Because the peptide influences cell migration and new blood-vessel growth — processes also relevant to tumor biology — people with a history of cancer are often advised to be especially cautious. None of this is medical advice: anyone considering TB-500 should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history, and competitive athletes should remember it is prohibited in sport.

TB-500 FAQ

What is a common TB-500 dose?

Commonly referenced protocols use 2-2.5 mg, twice weekly, often with a higher-frequency loading phase of a few weeks followed by a lower maintenance frequency. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

How do you reconstitute TB-500?

TB-500 is supplied as a lyophilized powder and mixed with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection. StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator gives you the exact units to draw for your vial size and target dose on a U-100 insulin syringe.

Is TB-500 the same as thymosin beta-4?

Not exactly. Thymosin beta-4 is a naturally occurring 43-amino-acid protein. TB-500 is a synthetic peptide built around the active, actin-binding region of that protein, which is why the two are discussed together and often used interchangeably in peptide communities.

What is TB-500 commonly stacked with?

BPC-157 is the most frequently paired compound. The two are often described as complementary for soft-tissue recovery — BPC-157 associated more with localized repair and angiogenesis, TB-500 with cell migration and flexibility across the whole body.

Is TB-500 FDA approved?

No. TB-500 is not an approved drug and its evidence base is largely preclinical animal research. It is not a treatment for any condition, and it is banned in competitive sport by WADA. Discuss any use with a qualified healthcare provider.

How long do people run TB-500?

A loading block of several weeks followed by a maintenance phase is commonly referenced, but there is no established clinical protocol because human trial data is limited. Any cycle length should be discussed with a provider.
