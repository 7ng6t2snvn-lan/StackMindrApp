Niacinamide (B3)

The non-flushing form of vitamin B3, also called nicotinamide. It feeds into the body's NAD+ pathway and is widely used for skin and general wellness — and unlike nicotinic acid, it does not cause the classic niacin flush.

What is niacinamide?

Niacinamide — also written as nicotinamide — is one of the two main forms of vitamin B3. The other is nicotinic acid (plain niacin). Both ultimately support the same B3-dependent chemistry in the body, but they behave quite differently in practice, which is the single most useful thing to understand about niacinamide.

The headline difference is the flush. Nicotinic acid can trigger a warm, red, tingling skin flush, especially at higher doses. Niacinamide does not; it is the form people reach for when they want B3 without that effect. It also does not share nicotinic acid's lipid-lowering use. Niacinamide is sold as an inexpensive oral supplement and is a common ingredient in skin-care serums.

What niacinamide does and why people take it

Inside cells, B3 is a building block for NAD+ and NADP, coenzymes involved in energy metabolism and DNA repair. This is why niacinamide is often grouped with NAD+ precursors like NMN and NR, though it works through its own salvage pathway. It is commonly referenced for:

  • Skin — topical niacinamide is one of the better-studied cosmetic ingredients, associated in trials with improved barrier function and the appearance of redness and uneven tone.
  • Cellular energy and repair — as an NAD+ input, it supports the coenzyme pool that powers metabolism, which is the basis of its popularity in longevity circles.
  • General B3 sufficiency — it reliably covers vitamin B3 needs and prevents deficiency.

Be honest about the evidence: the topical skin data is reasonably strong, but many of the systemic anti-aging and NAD+ claims rest on early or preliminary research. Niacinamide is a well-established vitamin, not a proven treatment for aging or disease.

How niacinamide is commonly used

Niacinamide is taken orally as a capsule or tablet, typically with food. It is water-soluble, so it does not require dietary fat for absorption. There is no reconstitution or injection involved — it is a simple oral supplement. For skin goals, it is also applied topically in serums rather than taken by mouth.

Dietary vitamin B3 comes from meat, poultry, fish, peanuts, and fortified grains, and the body can also make some from the amino acid tryptophan. Most people who supplement do so for skin or NAD+-related reasons rather than to correct a true deficiency, which is uncommon in well-fed populations.

Safety and considerations

At typical supplemental doses niacinamide is generally well tolerated and does not cause the niacin flush. The main caution is at the high end: sustained multi-gram daily intakes have been linked to liver enzyme changes, so very high doses are something to discuss with a clinician and potentially monitor. Some users notice sleep disruption when dosing late in the day.

As with any supplement, quality varies by brand, and niacinamide can interact with certain medications. None of this is medical advice — anyone with liver concerns, who is pregnant, or who takes prescription drugs should review niacinamide with a qualified healthcare provider first.

Niacinamide FAQ

Is niacinamide the same as niacin?

Both are forms of vitamin B3, but they behave differently. Niacinamide (nicotinamide) does not cause the skin flush and is not used to lower cholesterol, whereas nicotinic acid (plain niacin) can do both.

Does niacinamide cause the niacin flush?

No. The flush is associated with nicotinic acid. Niacinamide is the form people choose specifically to avoid that warm, red, tingling reaction.

Is niacinamide an NAD+ booster?

It is a vitamin B3 form that feeds the NAD+ salvage pathway, so it is often grouped with NAD+ precursors. The strength of its systemic anti-aging effects in humans is still an area of early research.

Is niacinamide safe?

At common supplemental doses it is generally well tolerated. Very high multi-gram daily doses have been linked to liver stress, so high intakes should be discussed with a healthcare provider. This is educational reference only.
