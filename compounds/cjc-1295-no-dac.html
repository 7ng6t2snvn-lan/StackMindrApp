What is CJC-1295 no DAC?

CJC-1295 no DAC is a synthetic analog of growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), built on the active 1-29 fragment of the natural hormone. It is often listed under its other name, Modified GRF 1-29, or "Mod GRF 1-29." A handful of amino-acid substitutions make the molecule more resistant to the enzymes that normally break GHRH down within minutes, so it survives a little longer and produces a stronger signal than unmodified GRF 1-29 — but it is still cleared quickly.

The "no DAC" part is the key to understanding it. The original CJC-1295 molecule included a Drug Affinity Complex (DAC) that binds the peptide to albumin in the blood, dramatically extending its half-life to days and creating a sustained "bleed" of growth hormone. The no-DAC version removes that feature. The result is a short-acting peptide that gives a sharp, discrete pulse of growth hormone and then clears — which many people consider more physiologic because it respects the body's natural on-off rhythm.

Like most peptides in this category, CJC-1295 no DAC is sold and discussed as a research compound. It is not an approved medicine, and it is not established as a treatment for any condition.

How CJC-1295 no DAC is thought to work

It acts upstream of growth hormone, at the pituitary, through the GHRH receptor:

  • GHRH-receptor agonism — it binds the same receptor as natural GHRH on pituitary somatotroph cells, stimulating the synthesis and release of growth hormone.
  • A clean, short pulse — because there is no DAC, its effect rises and falls quickly rather than producing a continuous elevation, so growth hormone is released in a burst that resembles the body's own pattern.
  • Preserved feedback — working upstream means somatostatin and IGF-1 feedback still regulate the system, which is thought to reduce the risk of the sustained, unnaturally high levels seen with exogenous HGH.
  • Downstream IGF-1 — the growth hormone it releases drives production of IGF-1, the mediator behind most of growth hormone's downstream effects on tissue and metabolism.

This is also why it pairs so well with a growth-hormone-releasing peptide: one class increases the amount of GH released per pulse, while the other triggers the pulse through a separate receptor.

What the research shows

The underlying biology of GHRH analogs is well established — GHRH's role in releasing growth hormone is textbook endocrinology, and the original long-acting CJC-1295 (with DAC) was studied in early clinical research for its ability to raise GH and IGF-1 levels. That work is where much of the public interest originated.

The no-DAC version specifically, however, lives almost entirely in the research-compound world. There are no large, high-quality human trials establishing the body-composition, recovery, or longevity benefits it is popularly associated with in healthy adults. The mechanism is sound and the GH-releasing effect is real, but the specific outcomes people chase are extrapolated rather than proven. Treat dramatic before-and-after claims with skepticism — the honest picture is "well-understood mechanism, thin human outcome data."

How CJC-1295 no DAC is commonly used

Commonly referenced protocols use around 100 mcg per dose, typically at bedtime and on an empty stomach. Because the peptide is short-acting, some protocols reference dosing more than once a day, each time away from meals — carbohydrate and fat can blunt the growth-hormone response. Bedtime dosing is favored because it stacks on top of the body's largest natural overnight GH pulse.

It arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water and injected subcutaneously with a small insulin syringe. Commonly stacked with

CJC-1295 no DAC and ipamorelin are the single most famous pairing in the GH-peptide world. The rationale is simple: CJC-1295 no DAC is a GHRH analog and ipamorelin is a growth-hormone-releasing peptide (GHRP), so they act on two different receptors and are described as synergistic — one increases the size of the pulse the other triggers. The combination is sold so often that it also appears as a pre-mixed CJC/Ipamorelin blend. Sermorelin is a closely related GHRH analog people frequently compare it to.

Side effects & safety considerations

In anecdotal reports and the limited available data, CJC-1295 no DAC is generally described as well tolerated. The most commonly mentioned effects are injection-site reactions (redness, itching), transient flushing or warmth, head-rush sensations shortly after dosing, and occasional water retention or tingling in the hands — the latter being familiar effects of raising growth hormone. Some people note vivid dreams or changes in sleep, consistent with dosing at night.

The honest limitations: there is no long-term human safety record for this specific peptide, and because it raises growth hormone and IGF-1, anyone with active cancer, a history of cancer, or conditions affected by IGF-1 signaling is generally advised to be especially cautious. Purity and dosing accuracy vary widely across research suppliers of an unregulated product. None of this is medical advice — CJC-1295 no DAC affects a hormonal axis and should only be considered under the supervision of a qualified healthcare provider who knows your history.

CJC-1295 no DAC FAQ

What is a common CJC-1295 no DAC dose?

Commonly referenced protocols use around 100 mcg per dose, often at bedtime and sometimes more than once a day because the peptide is short-acting. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

What is the difference between CJC-1295 with and without DAC?

DAC (drug affinity complex) binds the peptide to albumin so it lasts for days and produces a sustained GH "bleed." Without DAC — the Modified GRF 1-29 version — it is cleared within minutes to hours, so it produces a short, clean pulse of growth hormone that better mimics the body's natural rhythm.

Why is CJC-1295 no DAC stacked with ipamorelin?

CJC-1295 no DAC is a GHRH analog and ipamorelin is a growth-hormone-releasing peptide, so they act on two different receptors. Used together they are described as synergistic — one amplifies the size of the GH pulse that the other triggers — which is why they are the most famous GH peptide pairing.
