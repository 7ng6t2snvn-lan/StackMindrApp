What is VIP?

VIP, or Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide, is a 28-amino-acid peptide the body produces naturally. Despite the name — which reflects its discovery in the intestinal tract — it acts throughout the body as a signaling molecule, with roles in regulating blood vessels, immune activity, and the nervous system. It is one of the body's own broad-acting anti-inflammatory and regulatory peptides.

In clinical and wellness use it is most associated with protocols for Chronic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (CIRS) and mold-related illness, where it was popularized as a late-stage step after other treatments. It is supplied as a lyophilized powder that is reconstituted and administered as a nasal spray rather than by injection.

VIP in this form is not an FDA-approved treatment for CIRS or mold illness; it is used off-label and through compounding, and its evidence in that specific context is limited.

How VIP is thought to work

VIP acts on dedicated VIP receptors (VPAC1 and VPAC2) found on immune cells, blood vessels, and nervous-system tissue. The effects described in research include:

  • Anti-inflammatory signaling — shifting immune activity away from pro-inflammatory states, which is the basis for its use in inflammatory syndromes.
  • Vasodilation — relaxing blood vessels and smooth muscle, including in the airways, which relates to its respiratory effects.
  • Hormonal and regulatory effects — influence on pituitary and other regulatory signaling that CIRS protocols target.
  • Neuroprotective and circadian roles — VIP is involved in the brain's master clock and in protecting nervous-system tissue.

The intranasal route is used to deliver the peptide to the upper airway and nearby tissues directly.

What the research shows

As a natural peptide, VIP has a substantial basic-science literature covering its receptors, its anti-inflammatory actions, and its roles in the gut, lungs, and brain. There is also published work on its vasodilatory and immune-modulating effects in controlled settings.

The gap is between that general science and the specific intranasal CIRS protocol it is best known for, which rests largely on the clinical experience of the practitioners who developed it rather than on large independent randomized trials. So while VIP the molecule is well characterized, VIP the nasal-spray protocol is far less validated. That distinction is worth keeping in mind when evaluating strong claims.

VIP has also been investigated in other medical contexts — including inhaled formulations studied for pulmonary conditions — which further confirms that the peptide is biologically active and pharmacologically interesting. What that research does not do is establish that the intranasal wellness protocol reliably reverses the specific symptoms attributed to CIRS, which remains the central open question.

How VIP is commonly used

Commonly referenced protocols use roughly 50 mcg per dose administered intranasally, often several times daily, and typically only after specific prerequisites in the CIRS framework are met. It is a nasal spray, not an injection.

The emphasis on prerequisites is a defining feature of how VIP is used in this framework, and it is one of the more unusual aspects compared with other peptides. Rather than being a starting point, it is positioned as a final step taken only once other markers are in range, which is why it is rarely used casually or in isolation.

VIP is supplied as a lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted with sterile or bacteriostatic water before being loaded into a nasal spray device. Side effects & safety considerations

Because VIP is a potent vasodilator, the most commonly reported effects relate to blood pressure and circulation — flushing, lightheadedness, or a drop in blood pressure, particularly early in use. Some users report nasal irritation from the spray itself, and transient changes in mood or energy.

In the CIRS framework, practitioners emphasize that VIP should only be used after specific conditions are addressed, because using it too early is thought to be counterproductive. There is no robust long-term safety record for this off-label use, and compounded product quality varies. None of this is medical advice — anyone considering VIP should work with a qualified healthcare provider experienced in these protocols.

VIP FAQ

What is VIP (Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide)?

VIP is a naturally occurring 28-amino-acid neuropeptide with broad anti-inflammatory, vasodilatory, and regulatory roles. As a supplement it is used intranasally, most associated with CIRS and mold-illness protocols.

How does VIP work?

It acts on VIP receptors on immune cells, blood vessels, and nerve tissue, shifting immune activity away from inflammation, relaxing blood vessels and airways, and supporting regulatory and neuroprotective signaling.

Is VIP injected?

No, in these protocols it is used as an intranasal spray. It is supplied as a lyophilized powder, reconstituted, and loaded into a nasal spray device rather than injected.

Is VIP FDA approved for CIRS?

No. Intranasal VIP for CIRS or mold illness is used off-label and through compounding, and its evidence in that specific context comes largely from practitioner experience rather than large trials.
