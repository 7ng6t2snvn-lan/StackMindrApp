What is LL-37?

LL-37 is an antimicrobial peptide that your body already makes. It is the active fragment of the only cathelicidin found in humans, released by immune cells and by the skin, gut, and respiratory lining as part of innate immunity — the fast, non-specific defenses that act before the adaptive immune system mounts a targeted response. Its name comes from its sequence: it begins with two leucine (“LL”) residues and is 37 amino acids long.

What makes LL-37 interesting to researchers is that it does more than kill microbes. It also helps coordinate the immune response and plays a role in wound healing, which is why it shows up in research spanning infection, inflammation, skin repair, and even conditions where it is over- or under-active.

As a supplement or research injectable, LL-37 is not an approved medicine, and the human evidence for administering it is limited. It is studied as a fascinating piece of our own immune biology far more than as a proven therapy.

How LL-37 is thought to work

LL-37 is a genuinely multifunctional peptide. The roles described in research include:

  • Direct antimicrobial action — disrupting the membranes of bacteria, and showing activity against some fungi and viruses in laboratory studies.
  • Immune modulation — acting as a signaling molecule that recruits immune cells and shapes the inflammatory response, both dialing it up and helping resolve it depending on context.
  • Wound healing and angiogenesis — supporting tissue repair and new blood-vessel formation in experimental models.
  • Biofilm disruption — interfering with the protective films some bacteria form, which is of interest for stubborn infections.

This breadth is a double-edged sword: LL-37's activity is highly context-dependent, and in some conditions (certain inflammatory skin diseases) too much LL-37 is thought to contribute to the problem rather than solve it.

What the research shows

LL-37 is heavily studied as a part of human biology — there is a large body of laboratory and mechanistic research characterizing its antimicrobial and immune-modulating roles. It has been investigated in the context of chronic wounds, infections, and various inflammatory conditions, and its dual role (protective in some settings, harmful when dysregulated in others) is well documented.

Where the evidence thins out is in using LL-37 as an administered therapy. There are no large human trials establishing that injecting supplemental LL-37 safely treats infections or speeds healing in people. So the honest framing is: rich science on what LL-37 does in the body, little clinical evidence for taking it as a product.

How LL-37 is commonly used

Commonly referenced protocols use roughly 50–100 mcg per dose, often daily, by subcutaneous injection. Some reports describe localized use near an area of interest. Because LL-37 is a research compound without an established clinical protocol, reported regimens vary.

Side effects & safety considerations

In research and anecdotal use, injection-site reactions are the most commonly mentioned complaint, and because LL-37 is a potent immune signaler, some people report flu-like or inflammatory responses. The context-dependence is the key nuance: LL-37 is implicated in driving certain inflammatory skin conditions, so the assumption that “more is better” does not hold for this peptide.

As with the rest of this category, there is no long-term human safety record for supplemental LL-37, and purity varies across unregulated suppliers. None of this is medical advice. Anyone considering LL-37 — particularly those with inflammatory or autoimmune skin conditions — should discuss it with a qualified healthcare provider.
