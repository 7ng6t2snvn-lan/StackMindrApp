What is liraglutide?

Liraglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist — a class of drugs that mimics the gut hormone glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1). It was one of the first long-acting GLP-1 agonists to reach the market and is best understood as the predecessor to semaglutide: the compound that proved out the approach the newer weekly agents then refined.

It is FDA approved and sold under two brand names for two indications. Victoza is approved for type 2 diabetes, where it helps improve blood-sugar control; Saxenda is approved for chronic weight management, at a higher dose. Both are the same molecule delivered as a once-daily subcutaneous injection. Because liraglutide has been on the market for years, it has an established clinical track record — one of the main things that distinguishes it from the many investigational compounds in the metabolic space.

How liraglutide works

As a GLP-1 receptor agonist, liraglutide reproduces the effects of the body's own GLP-1. The actions most relevant to blood sugar and weight include:

  • Glucose-dependent insulin release — it prompts the pancreas to release insulin when blood sugar is elevated, which lowers post-meal glucose without acting when sugar is already low.
  • Suppressing glucagon — it reduces glucagon, a hormone that raises blood sugar, further supporting glucose control.
  • Slowing gastric emptying — food leaves the stomach more slowly, prolonging fullness after meals.
  • Appetite regulation in the brain — GLP-1 signaling reduces appetite and food intake, which is the mechanism behind its weight effect.

These are the same core mechanisms that semaglutide and other GLP-1 agents use. The difference between the drugs is less about what they do and more about how long they last in the body.

Liraglutide vs. semaglutide: the key difference

The most important practical distinction is frequency. Liraglutide has a shorter half-life and must be injected once daily. Semaglutide was engineered with a much longer half-life, which is why it is given once weekly — a single injection instead of seven. For many people the weekly schedule is simply more convenient and easier to stick to.

There is also a difference in magnitude. In clinical trials, liraglutide produces meaningful weight loss, but the average loss is generally smaller than that reported for the weekly agents — semaglutide and tirzepatide. That doesn't make liraglutide obsolete: it is well established, long studied, and remains a standard option, particularly where a daily GLP-1 is preferred or appropriate. But it does explain why the newer weekly drugs have captured so much attention. Individual results vary, and the right choice is a medical decision.

How liraglutide is used

Brand-name liraglutide is supplied as a ready-to-use solution in a prefilled, multi-dose pen and injected subcutaneously once daily — commonly into the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm. Because the branded product is already a liquid solution, it does not require reconstitution the way lyophilized peptide powders do; you dial the dose on the pen and inject. Doses are typically started low and increased gradually over several weeks to reduce gastrointestinal side effects, under a provider's direction.

Compounded liraglutide also exists and is sometimes supplied in vials rather than pens. Whichever form is used, it is a prescription medicine meant to be managed with a healthcare provider. Commonly compared with

Liraglutide is generally used on its own rather than stacked with other compounds. Where it comes up most is in comparison: people weighing GLP-1 options line it up against semaglutide (the weekly successor in the same class) and tirzepatide (a weekly GLP-1/GIP dual agonist associated with larger average weight loss). Side effects & safety considerations

The most common side effects of liraglutide are gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and reduced appetite — which tend to be most noticeable when starting or increasing the dose and often ease with time. This is why dosing is titrated up slowly.

As a GLP-1 receptor agonist, liraglutide's approved labeling carries class warnings, including a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodent studies; it is generally not used in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2. Pancreatitis and gallbladder issues are also listed as potential concerns. None of this is medical advice — liraglutide is a prescription drug, and its risks and benefits should be reviewed with a qualified healthcare provider who knows your full history.
