What is HCG?

HCG stands for human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone most famous for being produced in early pregnancy — it is the hormone that pregnancy tests detect. Medically, though, it is valued for a different property: its structure is close enough to luteinizing hormone (LH) that it can bind the same receptors and act as an LH mimetic.

LH is the signal the brain's pituitary normally sends to the gonads — in men, it tells the testes to produce testosterone and to keep spermatogenesis running. Because HCG reproduces that signal, it can stimulate the testes directly, which is the basis of most of its uses in men's hormone health.

HCG is an FDA-approved medicine with established indications in both men and women, including certain forms of hypogonadism and fertility treatment. In the TRT world it is best known as the add-on used to counter one of testosterone therapy's predictable downsides.

How HCG is thought to work

HCG works by standing in for luteinizing hormone at the LH receptor:

  • Direct testicular stimulation — in men, it signals the Leydig cells of the testes to produce testosterone and helps keep the testes active, even when the brain's own LH output is suppressed.
  • Preserving testicular size and fertility — because it keeps the testes "switched on," it counteracts the shrinkage and the drop in sperm production that TRT tends to cause.
  • Ovarian signaling in women — in fertility care, it is used to trigger ovulation or support the luteal phase by acting on the same gonadotropin pathway.
  • Supporting the HPG axis — more broadly, it substitutes for part of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal signaling chain when that chain is interrupted.

In other words, where testosterone therapy replaces the end-product hormone, HCG works further upstream, keeping the gonads themselves engaged.

What the research shows

HCG's core medical uses are well established: it has long been used to stimulate testosterone and sperm production in men with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and to trigger ovulation in fertility treatment. In the context of testosterone therapy, clinical experience and supporting studies describe its use to help maintain testicular volume and preserve some fertility that TRT alone would suppress.

The honest nuance is that protocols vary and the evidence for some TRT-adjunct uses is more clinical-practice-based than drawn from large dedicated trials. It is also worth noting that HCG was historically marketed for weight loss — a use that is not supported by evidence and has been the subject of regulatory warnings. This page describes legitimate, supervised medical use and is not a substitute for your prescriber's guidance.

How HCG is commonly used

On TRT, commonly referenced doses fall around 250-500 IU, roughly twice weekly, used alongside testosterone to keep the testes stimulated. Doses for fertility or other indications are different and set by the treating clinician. HCG is dosed in international units (IU) rather than milligrams, which makes getting the reconstitution concentration right especially important.

HCG is supplied as a lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection, and it is generally kept refrigerated once mixed. Commonly used with

HCG is most often paired with testosterone cypionate (or another testosterone ester) on TRT, specifically to offset the testicular suppression testosterone causes. In fertility-focused or recovery protocols it may be combined with agents that stimulate the body's own hormone axis, such as gonadorelin. These combinations are individualized clinical decisions guided by bloodwork.

Side effects & safety considerations

Possible side effects include injection-site reactions, acne, mood changes, and fluid retention. Because stimulating the testes also increases testosterone — and some of that converts to estrogen — HCG can raise estrogen in men, bringing estrogen-related effects. In fertility settings, higher-dose use carries its own specific risks that are managed by the treating specialist.

HCG is a prescription hormone, and dosing should be matched to bloodwork and symptoms rather than copied from a generic protocol. It is not appropriate for everyone, and the discredited "HCG diet" is explicitly not a legitimate use. None of this is medical advice: HCG should be prescribed, dosed, and monitored by a qualified healthcare provider.

HCG FAQ

What is a common HCG dose?

On TRT, commonly referenced doses fall around 250-500 IU, roughly twice weekly, to help maintain testicular function. Doses for other medical uses differ. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

How do you reconstitute HCG?

HCG is supplied as a lyophilized powder and mixed with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection. What does HCG do on TRT?

HCG mimics luteinizing hormone (LH), the signal the body normally sends to the testes. On testosterone therapy that natural signal is suppressed, so HCG is used to keep the testes stimulated — helping preserve their size and some fertility that TRT alone tends to reduce.

Is HCG FDA approved?

Yes. HCG is an FDA-approved hormone with established medical uses, including hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and certain fertility indications in both men and women. Its use alongside TRT is common in clinical practice and should be supervised by a provider.

What are the side effects of HCG?

Possible effects include injection-site reactions, acne, mood changes, fluid retention, and, because HCG can stimulate estrogen production in men, estrogen-related effects. It should be used under medical supervision, and dosing adjusted to bloodwork and symptoms.

What is HCG commonly used with?

In TRT protocols it is most often paired with testosterone cypionate or another testosterone ester to offset the testicular suppression that testosterone causes. Other fertility-focused protocols may combine it with agents that stimulate the body's own hormone axis.
