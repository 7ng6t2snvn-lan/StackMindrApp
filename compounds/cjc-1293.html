What is CJC-1293?

CJC-1293 is a growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) analog — a synthetic, modified version of the body’s own GHRH, specifically its active GRF(1-29) fragment (the same fragment behind sermorelin). Its job is to prompt the pituitary gland to release the body’s own growth hormone, rather than supplying growth hormone directly.

It is worth being upfront that naming in this family is confusing. The closely related CJC-1295 comes in two forms: one “with DAC” (a drug-affinity complex that binds to albumin and dramatically extends its half-life to days) and one “without DAC” (also called modified GRF 1-29), which is short-acting. The “CJC-1293” label is used less consistently by suppliers and is generally described as a modified GRF analog engineered for more sustained GH release than the plain no-DAC version. Because the labeling is not standardized, it is best understood as a member of the CJC/GRF analog family, and it is a research-grade peptide — not an approved drug.

How CJC-1293 is thought to work

Like other GHRH analogs, it works upstream — at the gland — rather than replacing growth hormone:

  • GHRH receptor activation — it binds the pituitary’s GHRH receptor and signals it to release stored growth hormone.
  • Preserves pulsatility — GHRH analogs tend to amplify the body’s natural pulsing release of GH rather than flooding the system with a constant level, which is considered more physiologic.
  • Raises GH and IGF-1 — the resulting GH release raises downstream IGF-1, the mediator of many of growth hormone’s effects.
  • Modifications for stability — amino-acid substitutions are intended to protect it from rapid breakdown, extending its action compared with unmodified GRF(1-29).

Because it relies on your own pituitary, a GHRH analog’s ceiling is set by what your gland can produce — a built-in limit that distinguishes this approach from injecting growth hormone itself.

What the research shows

The broader CJC-1295 / GRF analog family is well characterized: the foundational pharmacology showing that these modified GRF analogs raise GH and IGF-1 and can sustain that elevation is established, and related GHRH analogs such as tesamorelin are FDA-approved for a specific medical use (HIV-associated lipodystrophy), which validates the overall mechanism.

CJC-1293 specifically, as a less-standardized label, has thinner dedicated evidence than the named CJC-1295 forms, and much of what is claimed about it is extrapolated from the family as a whole. It is used within performance, recovery and anti-aging communities rather than as an approved therapy, and human data for its specific long-term use and safety is limited. The honest summary is “the mechanism and family are well founded; this exact variant is under-defined.”

How CJC-1293 is commonly used

Commonly referenced figures are around 100-200 mcg daily, often at bedtime — timing chosen to align with the body’s largest natural GH pulse during early sleep. It is frequently paired with a GHRP (ghrelin-receptor agonist) for a larger combined effect, as discussed below.

Injectable CJC-1293 arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Commonly stacked with

CJC-1293 belongs to a well-defined neighborhood. CJC-1295 (no DAC) and Sermorelin are its closest GHRH-analog relatives, and Tesamorelin is the FDA-approved analog in the same class. In practice GHRH analogs are most often combined with a GHRP such as Ipamorelin — the logic behind the popular CJC / Ipamorelin blend — because the two act on different receptors for a larger GH pulse.

Side effects & safety considerations

GHRH analogs as a class are generally reported to be reasonably well tolerated, with the most common complaints being injection-site reactions, transient flushing, and sometimes water retention, tingling or headaches — effects broadly associated with raising GH and IGF-1. Because they increase growth signaling, standard cautions around GH apply, and anyone with a history of cancer or active tumors is typically advised to be especially careful.

Specific to CJC-1293, the inconsistent labeling is itself a safety consideration: you cannot be fully certain what a given vial actually contains, and research-supplier purity varies. It is not an approved drug and has no long-term human safety record at the doses people use. This is not medical advice; discuss any use with a qualified healthcare provider.

CJC-1293 FAQ

What is CJC-1293?

CJC-1293 is a modified growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) analog in the CJC-1295 / GRF(1-29) family. It prompts the pituitary to release the body’s own growth hormone rather than supplying growth hormone directly.

How is CJC-1293 different from CJC-1295?

Naming in this family is inconsistent. CJC-1295 comes with DAC (long-acting, lasting days) or without DAC (short-acting modified GRF 1-29). CJC-1293 is a less-standardized label generally described as a modified GRF analog aimed at sustained release.

How does CJC-1293 work?

It activates the pituitary’s GHRH receptor, amplifying the body’s natural pulsing release of growth hormone, which in turn raises IGF-1. Because it relies on your own pituitary, its effect is limited by what your gland can produce.

Is CJC-1293 FDA approved?

No. CJC-1293 is a research-grade peptide and is not approved. Related GHRH analogs such as tesamorelin are approved for a specific medical use, but the CJC peptides themselves are not.

What is CJC-1293 commonly stacked with?

It is most often combined with a GHRP such as ipamorelin, since the two act on different receptors to produce a larger growth-hormone pulse — the rationale behind the popular CJC / ipamorelin blend.

When is CJC-1293 usually taken?
