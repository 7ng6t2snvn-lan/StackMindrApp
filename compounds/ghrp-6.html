What is GHRP-6?

GHRP-6 stands for growth hormone-releasing peptide 6. It is one of the earliest synthetic growth hormone secretagogues and, in many ways, the template from which later peptides in this class were refined. Like its relatives, it prompts the pituitary gland to release a pulse of the body's own growth hormone rather than supplying synthetic GH from outside.

GHRP-6 works through the ghrelin receptor — the receptor for the body's natural hunger hormone — and that connection defines its signature trait: it stimulates appetite more strongly than almost any other peptide in the class. For someone who struggles to eat enough, that can be the point; for someone trying to lean out, it is usually a dealbreaker.

It is not an approved drug for general use, is sold as a research compound, and is banned in competitive sport. It is best understood as the older, hunger-heavy member of a family that now includes more selective options like GHRP-2 and Ipamorelin.

How GHRP-6 is thought to work

The mechanisms are well mapped for this class. The effects most often described include:

  • Ghrelin-receptor activation — GHRP-6 binds the GH secretagogue (ghrelin) receptor, driving a pulse of growth hormone from the pituitary.
  • Pronounced appetite stimulation — because it mimics ghrelin's action, it produces a strong, fast increase in hunger, often within minutes of dosing.
  • Natural pulsatile release — the GH it elicits follows the body's own pulse-and-recover rhythm rather than a flat elevation.
  • Modest effect on other hormones — GHRP-6 does raise cortisol and prolactin somewhat, though the headline feature remains its effect on appetite rather than those hormones.

As with other GHRPs, it is frequently paired with a GHRH analog, since the two classes act on different receptors and are described as working together for a larger GH release.

What the research shows

As an early and influential secretagogue, GHRP-6 has a reasonable research footprint confirming that it stimulates growth hormone release in humans, and it helped establish the whole concept of ghrelin-receptor GH secretagogues. Its appetite-stimulating effect is also well recognized and has prompted interest in contexts where boosting food intake matters.

What is far less established is evidence for the long-term lifestyle outcomes people pursue with it — muscle gain, recovery, body recomposition — with a clean chronic-use safety record. Raising GH acutely is demonstrated; durable physique or health benefits from ongoing use are not well proven. The honest framing is a real, well-characterized mechanism attached to under-studied long-term claims.

How GHRP-6 is commonly used

GHRP-6 is supplied as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that is reconstituted with bacteriostatic water and injected subcutaneously. Commonly referenced protocols use around 100-300 mcg per dose, two to three times a day, often timed to waking, pre-workout, and before bed. There is no official protocol behind these figures.

As with other secretagogues, dosing fasted is a common convention, since food — especially carbohydrates and fats — blunts the GH response; the strong hunger GHRP-6 produces makes the post-dose eating window easy to fill. Commonly stacked with

GHRP-6 is often compared and paired with its siblings. GHRP-2 is the close relative chosen when someone wants a strong GH pulse with less overwhelming hunger, while Ipamorelin is the selective option for minimal appetite and hormonal side effects. For a synergistic GH release, GHRP-6 is commonly combined with a GHRH analog such as CJC-1295 (no DAC), since the two classes act on different receptors.

Side effects & safety considerations

The most prominent effect to plan around is intense hunger, which can derail fat-loss goals and lead to overeating. Other commonly reported effects follow the class: water retention, tingling or numbness in the hands, mild rises in cortisol and prolactin, and injection-site reactions.

The broader caution applies to anything that raises growth hormone and IGF-1 — potential effects on insulin sensitivity and blood sugar, and the general principle that promoting growth signaling is inadvisable for anyone with a history of cancer. Purity varies across unregulated suppliers, and GHRP-6 is a prohibited substance in tested sport. None of this is medical advice — anyone considering GHRP-6 should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history.

GHRP-6 FAQ

What is GHRP-6?

GHRP-6 (growth hormone-releasing peptide 6) is one of the first synthetic growth hormone secretagogues developed. It prompts the pituitary to release the body's own growth hormone by acting on the ghrelin receptor, and it is especially known for strongly stimulating appetite.

Why does GHRP-6 cause so much hunger?

GHRP-6 acts on the ghrelin receptor, and ghrelin is the body's natural hunger hormone. That same action produces a pronounced spike in appetite, usually within minutes of dosing — which can be helpful for gaining weight and unhelpful if you're trying to cut.

What is a common GHRP-6 dose?

Commonly referenced protocols use around 100-300 mcg per dose, often 2-3 times daily. This is educational reference only and not a recommendation; there is no established clinical dose for lifestyle use.

How do you reconstitute GHRP-6?

GHRP-6 is supplied as a lyophilized powder and mixed with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection. StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator gives you the exact units to draw for your vial size and target dose on a U-100 insulin syringe.

How does GHRP-6 compare to GHRP-2 and Ipamorelin?

All three are ghrelin-receptor secretagogues. GHRP-6 causes the strongest hunger; GHRP-2 gives a strong GH pulse with somewhat less appetite but more cortisol and prolactin; Ipamorelin is the most selective, with minimal effect on hunger and other hormones.

Is GHRP-6 FDA approved?

No. GHRP-6 is not an approved drug for general use and is sold as a research compound. It is not a treatment for any condition, and it is banned in competitive sport.
