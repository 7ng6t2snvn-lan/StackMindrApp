What is AICAR?

AICAR (5-aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide, also called acadesine or AICA riboside) is a small molecule, not a peptide. It is a nucleoside that the body can convert into a compound resembling AMP — the “low energy” signal inside cells — which lets it switch on the enzyme AMPK.

AMPK is often described as the cell’s master energy sensor: it activates when energy runs low (as it does during exercise) and shifts the cell toward burning fuel rather than storing it. By switching AMPK on pharmacologically, AICAR earned the nickname “exercise in a pill” — the idea of capturing some of exercise’s metabolic effects without the workout.

It has been used as a research tool for decades and studied clinically in cardiac and other settings under the name acadesine, but it is not an approved endurance or weight-loss drug, and it is explicitly banned in competitive sport.

How AICAR is thought to work

Its effects trace back to AMPK activation and what that enzyme does downstream:

  • AMPK activation — mimics the low-energy state that normally occurs during exercise.
  • Increased fat oxidation — AMPK promotes the breakdown of fatty acids for fuel.
  • Glucose uptake — it can increase GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake into muscle independent of insulin, which is why it is also studied in metabolic contexts.
  • Endurance gene expression — in animal work, chronic AMPK activation shifted muscle toward a more endurance-oriented, fatigue-resistant profile.

A practical limitation runs through all of this: AICAR has a very short half-life and poor drug-like properties, so the doses needed to produce meaningful effects are large, which has been a major obstacle to real-world use.

What the research shows

The study that made AICAR famous showed that sedentary mice given the compound ran significantly longer than untreated mice — an apparent endurance boost without training — and that effect was amplified when it was combined with an exercise-mimetic partner compound. That work is the origin of the “exercise in a pill” framing.

Translating this to humans is where it falls down. There are no robust human trials establishing AICAR as a safe or practical endurance or fat-loss aid; the required doses are impractical, and its metabolic effects in people are not well characterized for this purpose. It is better understood as a valuable laboratory tool for studying AMPK than as a usable performance compound. Its demonstrated potency is also exactly why anti-doping authorities prohibit it.

How AICAR is commonly used

There is no established consumer protocol. Research figures are weight-based — on the order of 10-50 mg/kg — which reflects laboratory dosing, not a recommendation, and the amounts implied are far larger than typical peptide doses because of the short half-life.

Injectable AICAR, when used in research, arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Side effects & safety considerations

Because AICAR acts on a fundamental, system-wide energy pathway, its effects are not confined to muscle. Human safety for endurance or fat-loss use is not established, and the very large doses its short half-life demands raise obvious concern. Early clinical work with acadesine also examined effects on heart tissue and blood cells, underscoring that it is not a benign “supplement.”

It is prohibited by WADA, so for any competitive athlete it is disqualifying. On top of that are the familiar unknowns of a research chemical: variable purity, no long-term human data, and no medical oversight. This is not medical advice; anyone considering AICAR should discuss it with a qualified healthcare provider.

AICAR FAQ

What is AICAR?

AICAR (acadesine) is a small-molecule nucleoside — not a peptide — that activates AMPK, the cell’s energy-sensing enzyme. It is studied as an “exercise mimetic” for endurance and fat oxidation.

How does AICAR work?

It mimics the low-energy signal that normally rises during exercise, switching on AMPK. That increases fat oxidation and insulin-independent glucose uptake and, in animals, shifts muscle toward an endurance profile.

Is AICAR banned?

Yes. AICAR is on the WADA prohibited list because of its demonstrated performance-related effects, so it is disqualifying for competitive athletes.

Why does AICAR need such high doses?

It has a very short half-life and poor drug-like properties, so large amounts are required to produce meaningful effects — one of the main reasons it has not become a practical human performance compound.

Is AICAR FDA approved?

No. AICAR is a research compound (studied clinically as acadesine in other contexts) and is not an approved endurance or weight-loss drug.
