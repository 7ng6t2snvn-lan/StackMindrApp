What is cagrilintide?

Cagrilintide is a synthetic, long-acting analog of amylin — a hormone co-secreted with insulin by the beta cells of the pancreas after a meal. Amylin works alongside insulin to help regulate how quickly food leaves the stomach and to signal fullness to the brain. Natural amylin breaks down quickly, which limits its usefulness as a drug; cagrilintide is engineered with an acylation (a fatty-acid chain attached to the peptide) that dramatically extends its half-life, allowing a once-weekly schedule.

In pharmacological terms, cagrilintide acts as an amylin receptor agonist — it binds and activates the receptors that amylin normally talks to. Because of this it is being developed primarily as a weight-management agent, and it has drawn particular attention as the amylin half of CagriSema, an investigational once-weekly combination with the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide.

The honest framing is that cagrilintide is still investigational. It is being studied in clinical trials, including large obesity and type 2 diabetes programs, but it is not approved as a standalone medicine and is not established as a treatment for any condition.

How cagrilintide is thought to work

Cagrilintide mimics the actions of amylin, and those actions overlap with — but are distinct from — what GLP-1 drugs do. The effects most often described in the research include:

  • Slowing gastric emptying — food leaves the stomach more slowly, which prolongs the feeling of fullness after eating and can blunt post-meal blood-sugar spikes.
  • Increasing satiety and reducing appetite — amylin signaling in the hindbrain is associated with meal termination and reduced food intake, so people tend to feel satisfied with less.
  • Complementing GLP-1 action — amylin and GLP-1 act through different receptors and pathways, so combining an amylin analog with a GLP-1 agonist is described as hitting appetite regulation from two angles at once.

This complementary mechanism is the central idea behind CagriSema: pairing cagrilintide with semaglutide is intended to produce a larger, more durable effect on appetite and body weight than either component alone. How fully these effects translate across different populations is still being characterized in ongoing trials.

What the research shows

Cagrilintide has moved through clinical development as both a standalone candidate and as part of the CagriSema combination. Early and mid-stage trials explored its effect on body weight in people with overweight or obesity, and the CagriSema program extended that work into larger phase 3 studies in obesity and type 2 diabetes.

What's fair to say is that the amylin pathway is a genuine, well-characterized target in metabolic biology, and cagrilintide is one of the most advanced long-acting amylin analogs in development. What's not yet settled is its final place in treatment: as an investigational agent, its long-term safety profile and its performance relative to approved weekly agents are still being established. Be cautious with any source quoting precise percentage weight-loss figures as if they were final — trial results have been reported in stages and should be read in context rather than treated as a fixed label claim.

How cagrilintide is commonly used

In clinical trials cagrilintide is a once-weekly subcutaneous injection. In its investigational and compounded form it is typically supplied as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before it can be drawn up and injected.

Commonly stacked with

Cagrilintide's defining pairing is with semaglutide — together they form CagriSema, the fixed-dose amylin-plus-GLP-1 combination at the center of its development. People exploring the metabolic space also reference multi-receptor agonists like tirzepatide (GLP-1/GIP) and the investigational retatrutide (GLP-1/GIP/glucagon), which pursue a similar goal — broader appetite and metabolic effects — by hitting several receptors with a single molecule rather than combining two drugs.

Side effects & safety considerations

Like other agents that act on appetite and gastric emptying, cagrilintide is most associated with gastrointestinal effects — nausea, reduced appetite, and other digestive complaints, which tend to be most noticeable early and when a dose is increased. These are the same broad category of effects reported across the GLP-1 and amylin classes.

The key limitation is that cagrilintide is investigational: there is no long-term, real-world safety record of the kind that exists for drugs that have been on the market for years, and grey-market or compounded material carries additional uncertainty around purity and labeling. None of this is medical advice. Anyone considering cagrilintide — on its own or as part of a combination — should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history, rather than relying on community reports.

Cagrilintide FAQ

What is cagrilintide?

Cagrilintide is a long-acting, acylated analog of the hormone amylin. It acts as an amylin receptor agonist and is being studied for weight management, most often in combination with semaglutide as a fixed-dose product called CagriSema. It is investigational and not approved as a standalone drug.

How is cagrilintide different from a GLP-1 like semaglutide?

Cagrilintide mimics amylin rather than GLP-1. Both slow gastric emptying and promote fullness, but they act through different receptors, which is why they are often studied together — the amylin and GLP-1 pathways are described as complementary for appetite control.

What is CagriSema?

CagriSema is an investigational fixed-dose combination of cagrilintide and semaglutide delivered in a single once-weekly injection. It pairs an amylin analog with a GLP-1 receptor agonist and has been studied in obesity and type 2 diabetes trials.

How is cagrilintide administered?

Cagrilintide is a once-weekly subcutaneous injection. Research and compounded material is often supplied as a lyophilized powder that is reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator gives the exact units to draw on a U-100 insulin syringe.

What is cagrilintide commonly stacked with?

It is most associated with semaglutide — the two are combined as CagriSema. People interested in the metabolic space also reference tirzepatide and retatrutide, which hit multiple incretin receptors in a single molecule.

Is cagrilintide FDA approved?

No. Cagrilintide is investigational and is not approved as a standalone medicine. Its evidence comes from clinical trials still in progress, including the CagriSema combination program. It is not a treatment for any condition.
