What is Larazotide?

Larazotide (also called larazotide acetate, or by its development code AT-1001) is a short peptide designed to work inside the gut and nowhere else. Its job is to regulate the tight junctions — the protein seals between the cells that line the intestine. When those junctions loosen, the gut becomes more permeable, letting through substances that would normally be kept out; this is the mechanism behind what is popularly called “leaky gut.”

Unlike most of the peptides in this glossary, larazotide is taken orally and is not injected. It is deliberately minimally absorbed, so it acts locally on the gut lining rather than circulating through the body. It is also unusual in that it has been a genuine pharmaceutical development candidate, carried into human clinical trials for celiac disease.

Larazotide is best described as an investigational drug. It is not FDA approved, and while it has more formal human study behind it than most research peptides, it has not completed the path to approval.

How Larazotide is thought to work

Its mechanism is narrow and well-defined compared with many peptides:

  • Tightening tight junctions — larazotide is thought to promote the reassembly of the junction proteins between intestinal cells, reducing permeability.
  • Blocking zonulin-driven opening — it acts as an antagonist in the zonulin pathway, the signaling system that loosens those junctions (notably in response to gluten in celiac disease).
  • Acting locally — because it stays in the gut and is barely absorbed, its effects are concentrated at the intestinal barrier rather than systemic.

The practical idea is that by keeping the barrier tighter, larazotide may blunt the immune and inflammatory response that follows when the gut becomes leaky — which is why it was developed specifically as a potential adjunct for celiac disease.

What the research shows

Larazotide is one of the more seriously studied compounds in this category. It has been evaluated in human clinical trials as an add-on to the gluten-free diet in celiac disease, aiming to reduce symptoms in patients who still react despite dietary control. Some earlier trials reported symptom improvements, which is what advanced it through development.

However, later-stage trials did not consistently meet their primary endpoints, and the program has not resulted in an approved product. So the accurate picture is: larazotide has real, formal human research — more than most peptides here — but the results have been mixed, and it is not a proven or approved treatment for celiac disease, leaky gut, or anything else.

How Larazotide is commonly used

In the clinical trials, larazotide was taken orally before meals — the timing matters because its goal is to keep the gut barrier tight during the exposure that comes with eating. Commonly referenced figures land around 0.5–1 mg, up to three times daily before meals, mirroring the trial dosing.

Because larazotide is an oral peptide, there is no reconstitution and no injection involved — it is simply taken by mouth, so the usual peptide mixing and syringe math do not apply. Side effects & safety considerations

Because larazotide is minimally absorbed and acts locally in the gut, it was generally reported as well tolerated in its clinical trials, with side effects broadly similar to placebo in many studies. That local-action design is one of its more reassuring features compared with systemic peptides.

Still, larazotide remains an investigational drug that is not approved, its trial results were mixed, and material sold outside a clinical setting varies in purity and is not quality-controlled as a medicine. None of this is medical advice. Anyone considering larazotide — especially for celiac disease, which requires proper medical management — should work with a qualified healthcare provider.

Larazotide FAQ

What is Larazotide used for?

It has been studied mainly as an add-on to the gluten-free diet in celiac disease, and more broadly for intestinal permeability (leaky gut), by tightening the junctions between gut-lining cells.

Is Larazotide injected?

No. Larazotide is an oral peptide taken by mouth before meals. It is deliberately minimally absorbed so it acts locally in the gut, so there is no reconstitution and no injection involved.

What is a common Larazotide dose?

Clinical trials used roughly 0.5–1 mg up to three times daily, taken before meals. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Why is Larazotide taken before meals?

Its goal is to keep the gut barrier tight during the exposure that comes with eating, particularly to gluten in celiac disease, so timing it just before meals is central to how it was studied.

Is Larazotide FDA approved?

No. Larazotide is an investigational drug. It has genuine human trial data, but later-stage trials had mixed results and it has not been approved for any use.
