What is Humanin?

Humanin is a small peptide, around 24 amino acids long, with an unusual origin: it is encoded within the genome of the mitochondria — the energy-producing compartments inside cells — rather than in the main nuclear DNA. It was one of the first members of a family now called mitochondrial-derived peptides, and its discovery helped open up the idea that mitochondria send out signaling molecules that act across the whole body.

It first drew attention in research on Alzheimer's disease, where it appeared to protect nerve cells from certain forms of stress. Since then it has been studied in the broader context of aging, metabolism, and cellular resilience. Levels of humanin tend to decline with age, which is part of why it is discussed in longevity circles.

As with most research peptides, the honest framing matters: humanin is studied as a biological signaling molecule, but as an injectable product it is not an approved medicine and is not established as a treatment for any condition. The human evidence is early.

How Humanin is thought to work

The effects attributed to humanin in the research literature are mostly protective and metabolic:

  • Cytoprotection — shielding cells, especially neurons, from apoptosis (programmed cell death) triggered by various stressors in experimental models.
  • Metabolic signaling — reported in animal studies to improve insulin sensitivity and influence glucose handling.
  • Mitochondrial and stress-response support — acting as part of a signaling system that helps cells cope with oxidative and metabolic stress.

Because these pathways touch the nervous system, metabolism, and cellular aging, humanin is often grouped with other “cellular health” peptides. The mechanisms are characterized largely in cell and animal systems, and how they translate to meaningful effects in people is not yet well established.

What the research shows

In laboratory and animal studies, humanin has been reported to protect neurons against amyloid-beta toxicity, to improve markers of insulin sensitivity, and to reduce cell death in models of ischemia and oxidative stress. Observational work in humans has noted that higher circulating humanin is associated with certain markers of healthier aging, and that levels fall as people get older.

What does not yet exist is a body of large, controlled human trials showing that supplementing humanin produces clinical benefits. The picture is “biologically interesting and protective in the lab, unproven as a therapy in people.” Claims that humanin reverses aging or treats neurodegenerative disease go well beyond the evidence.

How Humanin is commonly used

Commonly referenced protocols use roughly 1–5 mg per dose, often daily, administered by subcutaneous injection. Because humanin is a research compound without an established clinical protocol, reported regimens vary a great deal.

Humanin is supplied as a lyophilized powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before injection. Commonly stacked with

Humanin is most often discussed alongside other mitochondrial and longevity peptides. MOTS-c is its closest conceptual sibling — another mitochondrial-derived peptide with a metabolic focus — and the mitochondrial-targeted peptide SS-31 comes up in the same cellular-energy context. The more potent analog humanin analog (HNG) is the engineered variant of humanin itself.

Side effects & safety considerations

Humanin is an endogenous peptide — the body makes it naturally — and in research settings it has generally been described as well tolerated, with injection-site reactions being the most commonly mentioned issue. There is no well-characterized side-effect profile for supplemental use simply because the controlled human data does not exist.

That absence is itself the main caution: there is no long-term human safety record for injected humanin, and purity varies across unregulated suppliers. None of this is medical advice. Anyone considering humanin should discuss it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history.

Humanin FAQ

What is a common Humanin dose?

Commonly referenced protocols use roughly 1–5 mg daily by subcutaneous injection. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

What is a mitochondrial-derived peptide?

It is a peptide encoded within mitochondrial DNA rather than the cell's nuclear DNA. Humanin was one of the first identified, and these peptides are studied as signaling molecules involved in metabolism and cellular stress resistance.

How do you reconstitute Humanin?

Is Humanin the same as the humanin analog?

No. The humanin analog (HNG, or S14G-Humanin) is an engineered version designed to be more potent and longer-acting than natural humanin. They share the same rationale but are different molecules.

Is Humanin FDA approved?

No. Humanin is a research peptide, not an approved drug, and the human evidence is early. It is not a treatment for any condition.

What is Humanin studied for?

Research has explored neuroprotection, insulin sensitivity, and protection of cells against various forms of stress, mostly in laboratory and animal models rather than large human trials.
