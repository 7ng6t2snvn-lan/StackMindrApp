What is amycretin?

Amycretin is an experimental medicine from Novo Nordisk being developed for obesity. Its defining feature is that it is a single molecule that engages two different receptors: the GLP-1 receptor and the amylin receptor. Drugs built this way — one compound, two targets — are described as "unimolecular" dual agonists.

That combination is the interesting part. GLP-1 is the pathway behind semaglutide; amylin is a separate satiety hormone and the pathway targeted by cagrilintide. Rather than injecting two drugs, amycretin is designed to recruit both appetite-regulating systems from one molecule. Novo Nordisk has pursued it in both an oral form and a subcutaneous injectable form.

The honest status matters here: amycretin is early-stage and investigational. It is in clinical trials, it has not been approved by any regulator, and it is not available by prescription. It is also not something with an established research-chemical supply — availability is effectively limited to its trials, and anything sold under the name elsewhere cannot be assumed genuine.

How amycretin is thought to work

Amycretin is designed to combine two satiety signals in one compound:

  • GLP-1 receptor activation — associated with increased fullness, slower gastric emptying, and improved blood-sugar handling.
  • Amylin receptor activation — amylin is a hormone co-released with insulin that also promotes satiety and slows gastric emptying through a distinct mechanism.
  • Dual pathway rationale — the idea is that engaging GLP-1 and amylin together may suppress appetite more effectively than either alone, which is the thesis driving a wave of GLP-1-plus-amylin candidates.

These are the mechanisms of the targets amycretin hits; how it performs in people is what its trials are built to answer.

What the research shows

Amycretin has been studied in early clinical trials, and those early readouts reported encouraging weight-loss signals — strong enough that the molecule became one of the more talked-about obesity candidates in development. That excitement is real, but so is the caveat: this is early data from small, ongoing studies, not a completed, approved evidence base.

Because the program is early and still reading out, it would be misleading to attach specific percentages, participant counts, or timelines as established facts. The accurate summary is: promising early signals, both oral and injectable formats under study, and no regulatory approval — meaning long-term efficacy and safety remain unproven.

How amycretin is being developed

Two routes are in play:

  • Oral form — a tablet, which would remove injections entirely if it reaches patients.
  • Subcutaneous injection — the familiar route for peptide-based metabolic drugs. An injectable peptide of this kind is typically supplied as a solution or as a lyophilized powder that would be reconstituted before use.

Related compounds

Amycretin's closest conceptual relative is cagrilintide, the long-acting amylin analog, since amycretin folds an amylin action into one molecule. Semaglutide is the pure GLP-1 reference point, and retatrutide represents the multi-receptor direction the field is moving in.

Side effects & safety considerations

As an early-stage investigational drug, amycretin has no established long-term human safety record. Based on the GLP-1 and amylin classes in general, the effects most commonly associated with these drugs are gastrointestinal — nausea, reduced appetite, and related digestive complaints, usually most noticeable as dosing increases.

The central safety point is specific to its status: the full risk profile of amycretin is simply not yet known, and that is normal for a drug this early in development. Any product sold under its name outside a clinical trial cannot be assumed to be the real compound or correctly dosed. Amycretin FAQ

What is amycretin?

Amycretin is an investigational medicine from Novo Nordisk. It is a single molecule designed to activate two receptors at once — the GLP-1 receptor and the amylin receptor — which is why it is described as a unimolecular GLP-1 plus amylin agonist. It is being studied for obesity.

How is amycretin different from semaglutide?

Semaglutide acts on the GLP-1 receptor alone. Amycretin is built to hit both GLP-1 and amylin receptors in one molecule, combining two appetite-regulating pathways. Amylin is the same pathway targeted by cagrilintide.

Is amycretin a pill or an injection?

Both are in development. Novo Nordisk has studied amycretin in an oral form and a subcutaneous injectable form. Neither is approved or commercially available.

Is amycretin FDA approved?

No. Amycretin is an early-stage investigational drug in clinical trials. It has not been approved by any regulator and is not available by prescription or as a research chemical in any established way.

Does amycretin work for weight loss?

Early clinical trials reported encouraging weight-loss signals, which is why amycretin has drawn attention. But the data is early and the drug is not approved, so its true long-term efficacy and safety are not yet established.
