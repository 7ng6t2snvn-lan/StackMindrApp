Selenium (Selenomethionine)

An essential trace mineral the body needs in small amounts for thyroid hormone conversion, the glutathione antioxidant system, and immune function. Selenomethionine is a well-absorbed form — but selenium has an unusually narrow safe range, so more is not better.

What is selenium?

Selenium is an essential trace mineral, meaning the body cannot make it and needs a small, steady supply from the diet. It is incorporated into a group of proteins called selenoproteins, which carry out its biological work. Because the amount the body needs is measured in micrograms, the gap between "enough" and "too much" is smaller than for most nutrients.

Supplements come in a few forms. Selenomethionine is an organic form that is well absorbed and is the one most commonly used. Selenium-enriched yeast is another food-like source, while sodium selenite is an older inorganic form. Dietary selenium content depends heavily on the selenium level of the soil where food was grown.

What selenium does and why people take it

Selenium's roles are well established nutritionally. The most commonly referenced reasons for interest in it are:

  • Thyroid — selenium-dependent enzymes convert the thyroid hormone T4 into its active form T3, which is why it comes up in thyroid-health discussions.
  • Antioxidant defense — it is a required part of glutathione peroxidase, a key enzyme that neutralizes oxidative damage.
  • Immune function — adequate selenium supports normal immune responses.

Honest framing: correcting a genuine selenium deficiency has clear benefits, but in people who already get enough, extra selenium has not reliably shown added benefit — and some large trials found no benefit or even downsides from over-supplementing. This is a nutrient where sufficiency, not loading, is the goal.

How selenium is commonly used

Selenium is taken orally as a capsule or tablet, usually with food, and no reconstitution or injection is involved. It is also present in many multivitamins, so it is worth checking total intake across products to avoid stacking doses unintentionally.

The classic food source is Brazil nuts, which can be very high in selenium — sometimes enough that a couple of nuts cover daily needs — though content varies widely between batches. Seafood, organ meats, eggs, and grains also contribute. Because of soil variability and the Brazil-nut wildcard, some people prefer a measured supplement for consistency.

Safety and considerations

The defining safety point for selenium is its narrow window. Adequate intake is beneficial, but chronic excess causes selenosis — hair loss, brittle or white-spotted nails, a garlic breath odor, digestive upset, and in severe cases neurological symptoms. For this reason the tolerable upper intake is set around 400 mcg/day, counting all sources including food and multivitamins.

If you supplement long term, testing selenium status is reasonable, and combining a high-selenium diet with high-dose supplements is where people get into trouble. This is educational reference only; discuss selenium with a qualified healthcare provider, particularly if you have thyroid disease.

Selenium FAQ

What is selenium good for?

It is an essential trace mineral needed for converting thyroid hormone T4 to active T3, for the glutathione antioxidant system, and for immune function. Correcting a deficiency has clear benefits.

Can you take too much selenium?

Yes. Selenium has a narrow safe range. Chronic intake above roughly 400 mcg/day can cause selenosis, including hair loss, brittle nails, and neurological symptoms. Count food and multivitamin sources too.

Do Brazil nuts have selenium?

Yes, often a lot, but the amount varies widely between batches. That variability is one reason some people choose a measured supplement for consistency.

Which form of selenium is best?

Selenomethionine is a well-absorbed organic form and the most commonly used. Selenium-enriched yeast and sodium selenite are alternatives. This is educational reference only.
