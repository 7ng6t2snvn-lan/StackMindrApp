What is PE-22-28?

PE-22-28 is a short synthetic peptide derived from spadin, a natural peptide generated from the propeptide of the sortilin protein. Spadin attracted scientific attention because it blocks a specific potassium channel called TREK-1, and blocking that channel produced antidepressant-like effects in laboratory animals. PE-22-28 is an analog designed to keep that activity in a more usable form.

The headline that drives interest is speed: in rodent studies, spadin-type TREK-1 blockers produced antidepressant-like changes within a few days, compared with the several weeks that conventional SSRIs typically take to work. That raised hope for a faster-acting class of mood compounds.

As with most of this category, the framing has to be careful: the evidence is preclinical. PE-22-28 has not been through human clinical trials, is not an approved medication, and is not a treatment for depression or any other condition.

How PE-22-28 is thought to work

The proposed mechanism is fairly specific compared with broad-spectrum antidepressants. Effects described in the research literature include:

  • TREK-1 inhibition — blocking the TREK-1 background potassium channel, which is linked to mood regulation; TREK-1 knockout animals show a “depression-resistant” profile.
  • Enhanced serotonergic signaling — TREK-1 blockade is associated with increased activity in serotonin-related circuits.
  • Hippocampal neurogenesis — reported increases in new neuron formation, a change also seen with slower-acting antidepressants.
  • Rapid onset — the distinguishing feature in animal work is the short time to effect relative to SSRIs.

These pathways explain why PE-22-28 is discussed as a potential fast antidepressant, but they are characterized in animals and have not been confirmed in people.

What the research shows

In mouse and rat models of depression, spadin and its analogs including PE-22-28 have been reported to reduce depression-like behavior quickly and to increase markers of neurogenesis. This is the basis for the “four-day onset” figure that circulates online — it comes from animal behavioral studies, not human results.

There are no published human clinical trials of PE-22-28 for depression or mood. It is one of several TREK-1-targeting ideas that have generated academic interest precisely because a genuinely fast-acting antidepressant would be a meaningful advance — but interest and validation are different things, and this compound has not made the jump to human testing. The honest summary is that it is an interesting preclinical lead, not a proven therapy. Treat any claim that it reliably treats depression in people as unsupported by current evidence.

How PE-22-28 is commonly used

Community protocols reference 400-800 mcg daily by subcutaneous injection. These figures come from informal practice, not clinical trials, and there is no validated human dose or schedule.

PE-22-28 is supplied as a lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before injection. The correct draw volume depends on your vial size, the water added, and your target dose — StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator calculates that and shows the exact units on a U-100 insulin syringe. Given the absence of human data, any use should only be considered with guidance from a qualified provider.

Once reconstituted, the solution is typically refrigerated, kept out of light, and used within a limited window, as peptides break down over time in water. It is also worth being realistic about the measurement problem: because mood and motivation fluctuate for many reasons, a short, unblinded trial of an experimental peptide is easy to misread. Anyone exploring PE-22-28 is better served logging dose, timing, and consistent mood measures over a defined period than drawing conclusions from a few good or bad days.

Commonly stacked with

Within mood and cognitive interest, PE-22-28 is sometimes discussed alongside the Russian nootropic peptides Semax and Selank, which are associated with BDNF and anxiolytic effects respectively, and with P21 for its neurogenic profile. These are informal pairings, not studied combinations.

Side effects & safety considerations

Because PE-22-28 has not been tested in humans, there is no human safety data. Anecdotal reports are limited and may mention injection-site reactions, but nothing is established. Interactions with prescribed antidepressants or other psychoactive medications are completely uncharacterized and are a particular concern for a compound that targets mood pathways.

Depression is a serious medical condition, and nothing here should be read as a reason to delay or replace evidence-based care. Purity also varies across suppliers of an unregulated research chemical. None of this is medical advice: anyone considering PE-22-28 should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history.
