What is NAC?

NAC (N-acetylcysteine) is a stable, well-absorbed form of the amino acid cysteine. Its central role is as a building block for glutathione, often called the body's "master antioxidant" — a molecule that neutralizes free radicals and is essential to the liver's detoxification work. Because cysteine is usually the limiting ingredient in glutathione production, supplying NAC gives the body more raw material to make it.

NAC is not a fringe supplement; it is an established medicine. It is used in hospitals as the antidote for acetaminophen (paracetamol) overdose, where it protects the liver, and as a mucolytic that thins mucus in respiratory conditions. That genuine clinical pedigree is part of why it draws so much interest as a supplement.

What NAC does and why people take it

The thread through NAC's uses is glutathione and antioxidant capacity. People take it for liver support and detoxification, for respiratory health (its mucus-thinning effect is well established), and as a general antioxidant. There is also a notable body of research on NAC in psychiatry: it modulates glutamate signaling, and studies have explored it for conditions involving compulsive behavior, including OCD-spectrum symptoms and substance cravings, with mixed but intriguing results.

Honest framing matters given the breadth of claims. The acetaminophen-overdose and mucolytic uses are firmly proven. The psychiatric and addiction research is promising but not conclusive, and the broad "detox" and antiaging marketing outruns the evidence. NAC is a legitimately interesting molecule, but it is not a proven treatment for most of what it is sold for.

How NAC is commonly used

NAC is taken orally as a capsule — no reconstitution. A commonly referenced amount is 600–1,800 mg per day, often on an empty stomach for absorption and split into two doses at the higher end. It has a faint sulfur smell that some people notice. A practical regulatory note: NAC's over-the-counter status in the US has been contested at times, so availability can vary.

Safety & considerations

NAC is generally well tolerated. The most common complaints are gastrointestinal — nausea or upset stomach — especially at higher amounts, and the sulfur odor. Chronic high-dose use may lower zinc and copper over time, so people using it long-term sometimes monitor those minerals.

A few cautions: some people with asthma report bronchospasm or sensitivity, and because NAC can have mild blood-thinning effects it is worth flagging if you take anticoagulants or are approaching surgery. It should not be combined with nitroglycerin without medical oversight. Anyone who is pregnant or nursing, has asthma, or takes relevant medications should consult a qualified healthcare provider before use.

NAC FAQ

What is a common NAC dose?

Commonly referenced amounts are 600–1,800 mg per day, often on an empty stomach and split if higher. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

What does NAC actually do?

It supplies cysteine to make glutathione, the body's main antioxidant. It is a proven acetaminophen-overdose antidote and mucolytic; its antioxidant and psychiatric uses are researched but less settled.

Does NAC help with cravings or OCD?

There is intriguing research on NAC modulating glutamate for compulsive behaviors and substance cravings, but results are mixed and it is not an established treatment.

Is NAC safe?

It is generally well tolerated; the main issues are stomach upset and a sulfur smell. People with asthma, those on blood thinners, and anyone approaching surgery should take extra care.
