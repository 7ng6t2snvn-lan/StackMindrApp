What is NAD+?

NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is not a peptide or a drug — it is a coenzyme found in every living cell and one of the most fundamental molecules in human biology. Its job is to carry electrons in the chemical reactions that turn food into usable energy, and it is also a required fuel for a family of "repair and regulation" enzymes called sirtuins and PARPs.

The reason NAD+ shows up in longevity conversations is a well-documented observation: NAD+ levels decline with age. That decline is thought to contribute to flagging cellular energy and reduced repair capacity, so the idea of restoring NAD+ became one of the central hypotheses in aging research.

The chemistry is textbook-solid; the open question is whether topping up NAD+ in people produces the benefits the biology suggests. On that point, NAD+ is best described as a serious research target with experimental anti-aging claims, not a proven intervention.

How NAD+ is thought to work

The rationale for raising NAD+ rests on its two big roles in the cell:

  • Energy metabolism — NAD+ shuttles electrons through glycolysis and the mitochondrial electron transport chain, the core machinery that generates ATP. More available NAD+ is proposed to support more efficient energy production.
  • Sirtuin activation — sirtuins are enzymes tied to healthy aging, stress resistance, and metabolic regulation, and they literally consume NAD+ to do their work. Higher NAD+ is hypothesized to support their activity.
  • DNA repair (PARPs) — PARP enzymes also use NAD+ when repairing DNA damage, linking NAD+ availability to the cell's maintenance capacity.
  • Precursor conversion — oral NMN and NR don't act as NAD+ directly; the body converts them into NAD+ through the salvage pathway, which is why most oral products are precursors rather than NAD+ itself.

Together these explain why NAD+ is framed around energy, repair, and aging — it sits at the intersection of all three.

What the research shows

The basic biochemistry of NAD+ is not in doubt, and in animal studies, raising NAD+ (often with NMN or NR) has improved metabolic markers, mitochondrial function, and some measures of healthspan. Human trials of NMN and NR have generally shown that they can reliably raise blood NAD+ levels and appear reasonably safe at studied doses.

What human trials have not clearly shown is that this translates into the dramatic anti-aging, energy, or longevity benefits that marketing often implies. Results on performance, fatigue, and clinical endpoints have been mixed or modest. IV NAD+, popular in wellness clinics, has even less rigorous evidence behind it. The honest summary: NAD+ precursors can raise NAD+ and look safe so far, but "raises a biomarker" is not the same as "slows aging," and that gap is still open.

How NAD+ is commonly used

There are three routes, and they are quite different:

  • Oral precursors (NMN / NR) — the most common and convenient approach, with commonly referenced figures around 250-500 mg daily. NAD+ itself is poorly absorbed by mouth, which is why precursors are used.
  • IV infusion — delivers NAD+ directly into the bloodstream over a long, slow drip; users frequently report it feels intense (flushing, chest tightness, nausea) if run too fast, which is why clinics titrate the rate.
  • Subcutaneous injection — a middle-ground route some people use at home, dosed from a reconstituted vial.

For the injectable route, NAD+ may come as a powder that is reconstituted with bacteriostatic water, and getting the draw volume right depends on your vial size and dilution. Side effects & safety considerations

Oral NMN and NR are generally described as well tolerated in studies, with mild digestive upset being the most common complaint. The route that draws the most caution is IV NAD+: infused too quickly it commonly causes flushing, chest or abdominal tightness, and nausea — uncomfortable rather than dangerous for most people, but the reason clinics run it slowly.

The broader limitations are honest ones. NAD+ products are sold as supplements, not approved drugs, so quality and dosing aren't tightly regulated, and long-term safety at high doses isn't fully characterized. There is also ongoing scientific discussion about whether aggressively boosting NAD+ could have downsides in specific contexts, which is one more reason this isn't a casual DIY decision. None of this is medical advice — anyone considering NAD+, especially the IV or injectable routes, should involve a qualified healthcare provider.

NAD+ FAQ

What is a common NAD+ dose?

Commonly referenced figures are 250-500 mg daily for oral precursors like NMN or NR, with IV and injectable protocols using their own very different amounts. This is educational reference only — effective and safe dosing is not established, and it should be discussed with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is NAD+ taken orally, by injection, or IV?

All three are referenced. Oral products are usually precursors (NMN or NR) because NAD+ itself is poorly absorbed by mouth. IV infusions deliver NAD+ directly but are slow and can feel intense. Subcutaneous injection of NAD+ is also used as a middle ground. Routes differ a lot in how they feel and are handled.

What is the difference between NAD+, NMN, and NR?

NAD+ is the active coenzyme your cells use. NMN (nicotinamide mononucleotide) and NR (nicotinamide riboside) are precursors — building blocks the body converts into NAD+. Most oral supplements are precursors because they survive digestion better than NAD+ itself.

Does NAD+ actually slow aging?

NAD+ levels decline with age, and raising them improves markers in animal studies, which is why it is a serious longevity-research target. But human evidence that supplementing it slows aging or extends lifespan is not established. The anti-aging claims remain experimental.

Is NAD+ a drug or a supplement?

NAD+ and its precursors are sold as dietary supplements, not approved drugs, and are not a treatment for any condition. IV NAD+ is offered by some clinics off-label. Quality and dosing are not tightly regulated, so a provider's input matters.
