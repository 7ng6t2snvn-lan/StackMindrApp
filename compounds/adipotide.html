What is Adipotide?

Adipotide — sometimes written FTPP (fat-targeted pro-apoptotic peptide) — is an experimental peptidomimetic built from two joined parts: a homing sequence that recognizes the blood vessels feeding white fat, and a pro-apoptotic sequence that triggers cell death once it arrives. The concept is unusual: rather than suppressing appetite or speeding metabolism, it attacks the vasculature that supplies fat tissue, effectively starving fat deposits of their blood supply.

It came out of cancer-research work on targeting tumor blood vessels, which was then redirected at adipose tissue. It is strictly a research compound — it has never been an approved drug, and the human data is minimal.

How Adipotide is thought to work

The design is modular, and each part does a distinct job:

  • Vascular homing — the targeting sequence binds prohibitin, a protein found on the surface of blood vessels that specifically supply white adipose tissue.
  • Induced apoptosis — once docked, the attached pro-apoptotic motif disrupts the cell’s mitochondria, triggering programmed cell death in those vessel cells.
  • Fat regression — with their blood supply cut off, fat cells lose their support and the fat deposit shrinks. In animals this translated into rapid weight and fat loss.

The same targeting that makes it interesting also explains its danger: tissues such as the kidney are rich in blood vessels, and off-target effects there are the central safety problem.

What the research shows

The widely cited work is a primate study in which obese rhesus monkeys treated with adipotide lost a substantial fraction of body weight and showed meaningful reductions in fat mass over a roughly four-week course, alongside improved insulin sensitivity. Those results are what put the compound on the map.

The same research also reported the catch: dose-dependent kidney changes consistent with renal toxicity. That toxicity is the reason adipotide has not moved forward as a mainstream obesity treatment despite its dramatic efficacy. The animal work also suggested the effect was not permanent — fat tended to return once treatment stopped — which, combined with the kidney signal, undercuts the idea of it as a practical long-term weight solution. Human use is essentially experimental, with no established safety profile. The honest summary is “powerful in primates, but with a toxicity signal serious enough to stall it.”

How Adipotide is commonly used

There is no clinical protocol. The figures that circulate — around 0.43 mg/kg daily for a roughly 28-day course — come directly from the animal research, not from human dosing guidance, and weight-based research dosing does not map cleanly onto casual use.

Injectable adipotide arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder that has to be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Commonly stacked with

Adipotide is sometimes mentioned alongside other fat-loss research compounds, though they work through entirely different mechanisms. AOD-9604 and Tesofensine target fat metabolism and appetite respectively, while AICAR acts as a metabolic (AMPK) activator. None of these share adipotide’s vascular-targeting approach, and combining experimental compounds compounds the unknowns.

Side effects & safety considerations

The defining safety concern for adipotide is renal toxicity — kidney damage seen in animal studies that scales with dose. That alone places it in a different, higher-risk category than most recovery or GH peptides.

Beyond the kidney signal, it carries all the usual uncertainties of an unapproved experimental injectable: no human safety record, variable purity from research suppliers, and unknown long-term effects. Its mechanism — deliberately killing cells — is inherently less forgiving of error than compounds that merely modulate signaling. This is not medical advice; adipotide is a research compound, and anyone considering it should discuss the risks with a qualified healthcare provider.

Adipotide FAQ

What is Adipotide?

Adipotide (FTPP) is an experimental pro-apoptotic peptide that targets the blood vessels supplying white fat tissue, cutting off their blood supply so fat deposits shrink. It is a research compound, not an approved drug.

How does Adipotide cause fat loss?

A homing sequence binds prohibitin on fat-tissue blood vessels, and an attached pro-apoptotic sequence triggers cell death in those vessels. Without their blood supply, fat cells regress, which produced rapid fat loss in animals.

Is Adipotide safe?

It showed dose-dependent kidney toxicity in trials, which is the main reason it has not advanced as an obesity treatment. There is no established human safety profile, and it remains experimental.

Did Adipotide work in studies?

In obese primates it produced marked weight and fat loss and improved insulin sensitivity over about four weeks. However, the same studies reported renal toxicity, so efficacy came with a serious safety cost.

Is Adipotide FDA approved?

No. Adipotide is an experimental research compound and is not approved to treat any condition.

How is Adipotide administered?

Research used subcutaneous injection of a reconstituted powder, dosed by body weight over a short course. These are research figures, not a recommendation; dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.
