What is Ashwagandha?

Ashwagandha is a small shrub whose root has been used in Ayurvedic medicine for centuries. Its Latin name is Withania somnifera. In modern supplements it is sold as a standardized root extract, with KSM-66 and Sensoril being the two best-known branded extracts — KSM-66 is a root-only extract standardized to withanolides, while Sensoril uses root and leaf and is standardized to a higher withanolide percentage.

It is classed as an adaptogen, a loose category for plants thought to help the body cope with stress. Of all the adaptogens, ashwagandha is one of the more rigorously studied in human trials.

What Ashwagandha does / why people take it

The strongest and most consistent research signal is stress and anxiety reduction, generally tied to lower cortisol. Multiple randomized controlled trials report meaningful drops in perceived stress and in measured cortisol over 6–8 weeks, which is unusually good evidence for an herbal supplement.

  • Stress and cortisol — the best-supported benefit, with several positive randomized trials.
  • Sleep — some trials report improved sleep quality and faster sleep onset.
  • Testosterone and fertility in men — a few studies report modest increases in testosterone and improvements in sperm parameters; effects are moderate, not dramatic.
  • Strength and recovery — some resistance-training studies show small gains.

Honest framing: trials are often short and some are industry-funded, so effect sizes should be read conservatively. Benefits for stress are the most reliable claim.

How it's commonly used

Ashwagandha is taken orally. Commonly referenced intakes are 300–600 mg of a standardized extract per day, taken with food, either in the morning or split morning and evening. Because it can be mildly calming, some people prefer an evening dose for sleep. Standardized extracts are preferred over raw root powder for consistent withanolide content.

Safety & considerations

Ashwagandha is generally well tolerated over the typical trial length. The most common side effects are mild drowsiness and GI upset. A few important cautions apply: it can lower thyroid hormone needs and interact with thyroid medication, it may be stimulating to the immune system (a concern with autoimmune conditions), and it can add to the effect of sedatives.

There have been rare case reports of liver injury associated with ashwagandha products, which is part of why many people cycle it and favor reputable standardized extracts. It should be avoided in pregnancy. Anyone with thyroid, autoimmune, or liver conditions, or who takes medication, should consult a qualified healthcare provider before use. Ashwagandha FAQ

What's the difference between KSM-66 and Sensoril?

KSM-66 is a root-only extract standardized to withanolides and is often chosen for daytime energy and testosterone-related goals. Sensoril uses root and leaf with a higher withanolide percentage and is often chosen for sleep and relaxation.

How long does ashwagandha take to work?

Stress and cortisol benefits in trials are usually measured over 6–8 weeks of daily use. Some people notice calming effects sooner, but the studied benefits build over weeks.

Does ashwagandha raise testosterone?

A few studies in men report modest increases in testosterone and improved sperm parameters. Effects are moderate and the trials are short, so expectations should be measured. It is not a treatment for low testosterone.

Should you cycle ashwagandha?

Many people cycle off every 8–12 weeks because long-term human data is limited. There is no established requirement to cycle, but it is a common and cautious practice.
