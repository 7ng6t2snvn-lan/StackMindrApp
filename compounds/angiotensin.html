What is Angiotensin (1-7)?

Angiotensin (1-7), often written Ang-(1-7), is a small seven-amino-acid peptide that occurs naturally in the body as part of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) — the hormone network best known for regulating blood pressure and fluid balance.

Most people have heard of one side of that system: angiotensin II, which raises blood pressure, promotes inflammation and drives tissue scarring (fibrosis). Ang-(1-7) is the counter-regulatory arm. It is produced from angiotensin precursors largely by the enzyme ACE2 and generally opposes angiotensin II’s actions — relaxing blood vessels and acting as an anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory signal.

Because of that protective profile it has been investigated across cardiovascular, kidney, lung and cognitive research, and a pharmaceutical formulation has been studied in trials. It is still a research-stage compound and not an approved therapy.

How Angiotensin (1-7) is thought to work

Almost all of its effects are attributed to activation of the Mas receptor, a distinct receptor from the ones angiotensin II uses:

  • Vasodilation — it promotes nitric-oxide-mediated relaxation of blood vessels, lowering vascular resistance.
  • Anti-fibrotic signaling — it opposes the tissue-scarring effects of angiotensin II in the heart, kidney and lung in animal models.
  • Anti-inflammatory activity — it dampens several inflammatory pathways that angiotensin II amplifies.
  • Cognitive and neural protection — the Mas pathway is present in the brain, where Ang-(1-7) has shown protective and cognition-supporting effects in preclinical studies.

In short, it behaves like the “brake” to angiotensin II’s “accelerator” — which is the whole reason it attracts interest as a protective, longevity-oriented signal.

What the research shows

In animal models Ang-(1-7) has shown consistent cardioprotective, anti-fibrotic and vasodilatory effects, and the biology of the ACE2/Ang-(1-7)/Mas axis is well characterized and taken seriously in cardiovascular science. Pharmaceutical versions have been evaluated in human trials across several conditions, including cardiovascular and pulmonary settings.

Interest in this axis widened considerably when ACE2 — the enzyme that produces Ang-(1-7) — became a focus of respiratory-disease research, prompting trials of pharmaceutical Ang-(1-7) formulations in acute lung injury and related settings. That brought real clinical attention to the peptide, but it also underscores that it is studied as an investigational drug candidate, not validated for over-the-counter wellness use.

That said, human evidence for the longevity and general-wellness uses it is popularly associated with is still limited and early. The mechanism is credible and the preclinical data is encouraging, but it is not established as a treatment, and claims about specific anti-aging benefits should be read with appropriate caution. The honest framing is “solid biology, genuine clinical interest, but not yet a proven human intervention for most of what it’s marketed for.”

How Angiotensin (1-7) is commonly used

Commonly referenced figures sit around 200-400 mcg daily. Because the peptide is short-lived in the body, dosing is typically daily, and subcutaneous injection is the route most often described.

Side effects & safety considerations

As a counter-regulatory RAS peptide, Ang-(1-7) can in principle affect blood pressure and vascular tone, which is the most logical area of caution — particularly for anyone already taking blood-pressure medication or RAS-acting drugs, where effects could stack in unpredictable ways. Reported tolerability in research has generally been good, with injection-site reactions the most common minor complaint.

Beyond that, it shares the standard limitations of a research peptide: no long-term human safety record for wellness use and variable purity from research suppliers. Its influence on fundamental cardiovascular signaling makes medical oversight especially sensible. This is not medical advice; discuss any use with a qualified healthcare provider who knows your cardiovascular history.

Angiotensin (1-7) FAQ

What is Angiotensin (1-7)?

Angiotensin (1-7) is a naturally occurring seven-amino-acid peptide of the renin-angiotensin system. It acts on the Mas receptor and serves as the counter-regulatory, protective arm that opposes angiotensin II.

How does Angiotensin (1-7) work?

By activating the Mas receptor it promotes vasodilation and acts as an anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory signal, broadly opposing angiotensin II’s blood-pressure-raising and tissue-scarring effects.

What is Angiotensin (1-7) studied for?

Preclinically and in some human trials it has been examined for cardiovascular, kidney, lung and cognitive protection. The biology is well established, but human evidence for general wellness and anti-aging uses remains early.

Is Angiotensin (1-7) FDA approved?

No. It is a research-stage peptide, and the pharmaceutical formulations studied in trials are not approved treatments. It is not established as a therapy for any condition.

Does Angiotensin (1-7) affect blood pressure?

It can influence vascular tone and blood pressure as part of its mechanism, so it warrants particular caution for anyone on blood-pressure or RAS-acting medications. Discuss it with a healthcare provider first.

What is it commonly paired with?

It is discussed alongside other tissue-protective, anti-fibrotic peptides such as B7-33 and the broadly used recovery peptide BPC-157. These are educational comparisons, not a recommended protocol.
