What is methylfolate?

Methylfolate — L-5-methyltetrahydrofolate, or L-5-MTHF — is the active, circulating form of folate (vitamin B9). Folate is essential for DNA synthesis and repair, red blood cell formation, and methylation, and it is critical early in pregnancy for preventing neural tube defects. The reason the "methyl" form has a following is that it is the end product your body actually uses.

Dietary folate and synthetic folic acid both have to be converted through several enzymatic steps — the last one run by the MTHFR enzyme — before becoming active methylfolate. Some people carry MTHFR gene variants that reduce how efficiently they perform that conversion. Methylfolate skips the line by supplying the finished form directly, which is the central rationale for choosing it.

What methylfolate does and why people take it

People take methylfolate to meet folate needs, to support methylation (the process behind neurotransmitter production and DNA regulation), and specifically because of MTHFR variants. It is often paired with active B12 since the two work together in the same methylation cycle, and it comes up in conversations about mood, homocysteine, and prenatal nutrition.

A balanced view: for ordinary folate adequacy and for preventing neural tube defects, standard folate and folic acid are well proven, and the superiority of methylfolate for the general population is not firmly established. Where the active form has a clearer logic is in people with reduced MTHFR conversion. The widespread claims tying MTHFR variants to a long list of conditions are more enthusiastic than settled, so methylfolate is reasonable but not a cure-all.

How methylfolate is commonly used

It is an oral capsule or tablet — no reconstitution. A commonly referenced amount is 400–1,000 mcg, taken in the morning with food. A sensible practice is to start at the low end, because some people are sensitive to methyl donors and report overstimulation, anxiety, or trouble sleeping when they begin too high.

Safety & considerations

Folate is water-soluble and generally safe, and methylfolate is well tolerated by most people. The most commonly reported issue is over-methylation symptoms — irritability, anxiety, headache, or insomnia — in sensitive individuals taking higher amounts, which usually settle on lowering the dose.

One genuinely important point applies to all folate forms: high folate intake can mask the anemia caused by vitamin B12 deficiency while allowing B12-related nerve damage to progress undetected, which is why folate and B12 are usually considered together. People who are pregnant or planning pregnancy, who take methotrexate or anti-seizure medications, or who are managing a medical condition should set folate intake with a qualified healthcare provider.

Methylfolate FAQ

What is a common methylfolate dose?

Commonly referenced amounts are 400–1,000 mcg, taken in the morning with food, often starting at the low end. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

How is methylfolate different from folic acid?

Folic acid must be converted through several steps, the last by the MTHFR enzyme, before the body can use it. Methylfolate supplies the finished, active form directly.

Do I need methylfolate if I have an MTHFR variant?

It has a clearer logic for people with reduced MTHFR conversion, but the claims linking MTHFR variants to many conditions are not settled. Discuss it with a provider.

Why are folate and B12 considered together?

High folate can mask the anemia of B12 deficiency while nerve damage progresses, so they are usually balanced together.
