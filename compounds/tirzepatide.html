What is tirzepatide?

Tirzepatide is a dual incretin agonist developed by Eli Lilly. It is sold as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and as Zepbound for chronic weight management. What makes it distinctive is that a single engineered peptide activates two different receptors at once: the GLP-1 receptor (the same target as semaglutide) and the GIP receptor.

GLP-1 and GIP are both incretin hormones — signals the gut releases after eating that help the body manage a meal. Earlier metabolic drugs targeted GLP-1 alone. Tirzepatide was designed to recruit the GIP pathway as well, on the rationale that engaging both systems together produces a larger effect on blood sugar and appetite than either one on its own.

Like semaglutide, tirzepatide is a fully approved medicine with a large clinical-trial record, intended to be prescribed and monitored by a clinician. It is engineered to resist rapid breakdown, which is what allows once-weekly dosing.

How tirzepatide is thought to work

By activating two incretin receptors, tirzepatide reproduces several of the body's post-meal signals simultaneously:

  • GLP-1 activation — boosts glucose-dependent insulin release, lowers glucagon, slows gastric emptying, and reduces appetite through the brain's appetite centers.
  • GIP activation — adds a second incretin signal that is thought to further enhance insulin response and influence how the body handles fat and energy.
  • Combined appetite effect — the two pathways together are associated with a stronger reduction in hunger and food intake than GLP-1 alone, which is the main driver of weight loss.
  • Improved glucose control — the net effect on insulin and glucagon steadies blood sugar, the basis of its diabetes approval.

The precise contribution of the GIP arm is still an active research question, but the clinical result of the combination is well documented in the approved uses.

What the research shows

Tirzepatide's approvals rest on the large SURPASS trials in type 2 diabetes and the SURMOUNT trials in weight management. Across these programs it produced substantial average reductions in A1c and body weight, and in head-to-head diabetes comparison it outperformed a GLP-1-only agonist on those averages. More recent trials have also examined its effects in obstructive sleep apnea and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in people with obesity.

As with any medicine, averages are not guarantees — individual response varies, side effects lead some people to stop, and weight tends to return if treatment ends without other changes. The strength here is that these conclusions come from controlled human trials rather than anecdote. This page summarizes established medical use and does not replace your prescriber's guidance.

How tirzepatide is commonly used

Tirzepatide dosing is built around stepwise titration. Approved regimens begin at 2.5 mg weekly — a starting dose meant to let the gut adjust, not to be fully therapeutic — and step up over months toward a maintenance dose, to a maximum of 15 mg weekly. Dose increases are the point at which side effects usually peak, so clinicians generally hold at a tolerated dose before moving up.

The approved products are given as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection into the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm, on the same day each week. Commonly stacked with

Tirzepatide is used on its own and should not be combined with other incretin agonists. People researching it most often compare it with semaglutide (GLP-1 only) and the investigational triple agonist retatrutide. Running two drugs from this class together does not improve results and sharply raises the risk of severe gastrointestinal effects.

Side effects & safety considerations

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, constipation, and vomiting — and they are usually worst during dose increases, easing as the body adapts. Less common risks seen with the incretin class include gallbladder problems and pancreatitis, and low blood sugar when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.

Tirzepatide carries a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodents and is contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). It is not recommended in pregnancy and may reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives around dose changes. Because it is a prescription medicine, these considerations should be reviewed with a qualified healthcare provider. This page is educational and is not medical advice.

Tirzepatide FAQ

What is a common tirzepatide dose?

Approved regimens start at 2.5 mg weekly and are titrated upward in steps over months toward a maintenance dose, up to a maximum of 15 mg weekly. The starting dose is intended to limit nausea, not to be therapeutic. Exact dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is tirzepatide FDA approved?

Yes. Tirzepatide is an approved prescription medicine, sold as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and as Zepbound for chronic weight management. It should be used under medical supervision.

How is tirzepatide different from semaglutide?

Semaglutide acts on one receptor, GLP-1. Tirzepatide is a dual agonist that acts on both GLP-1 and GIP receptors. In head-to-head diabetes trials tirzepatide produced greater average reductions in blood sugar and body weight, though individual response varies.

Can you stack tirzepatide with other GLP-1 drugs?

No. Tirzepatide should not be combined with semaglutide, retatrutide, or other incretin agonists. Stacking agents from the same class does not improve results and sharply increases the risk of severe gastrointestinal side effects.

What are the common side effects of tirzepatide?

The most common effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, constipation, and vomiting — and they are usually worst during dose increases. Like other drugs in its class it carries a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rodents and is contraindicated with a history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2.
