What is Cortexin?

Cortexin is a low-molecular-weight polypeptide complex extracted from cerebral cortex tissue. Unlike the single short peptides in much of the bioregulator family, it is a mixture of small neuropeptides, and it is registered and used in Russia and several post-Soviet countries as a neuroprotective medicine — usually given as a short course of intramuscular injections.

That clinical history makes Cortexin somewhat better established than the more obscure bioregulators, but it has not been approved by the FDA and the independent, high-quality evidence remains limited. It is accurate to call it a widely-used regional clinical product rather than a globally validated therapy.

What Cortexin targets

The cerebral cortex is the brain's outer layer of grey matter, responsible for higher functions like attention, memory, language, and motor control. After an insult such as a stroke or head injury, the surrounding tissue goes through a vulnerable period, and the interest in Cortexin centers on supporting neurons through that window and over longer recovery. It is referenced in the contexts of stroke and traumatic brain injury rehabilitation, peripheral and central neuropathy, and cognitive maintenance. Where it is used clinically these are supervised indications; elsewhere they are simply what the compound is associated with, not proven outcomes.

How Cortexin is thought to work

As a tissue-derived peptide complex, Cortexin is proposed to act on brain tissue much as the wider bioregulator family is described — by supplying small peptides that influence the activity and maintenance of the cells of their source tissue. The specific actions most often attributed to it include:

  • A proposed neuroprotective effect, helping neurons withstand metabolic and oxidative stress.
  • Modulation of the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling in the cortex.
  • Support for the brain's own growth and repair factors during recovery.

These descriptions come largely from the research tradition around the product rather than from large independent trials, so they are best read as the proposed model for how it may act, not settled mechanism.

How Cortexin is commonly used

Cortexin is supplied as a lyophilized powder and reconstituted before intramuscular injection. A commonly referenced regimen is 10 mg once daily for roughly 10 days, sometimes repeated as a second course later. Safety & what we don't know

Where Cortexin is used clinically it is generally described as well tolerated, with injection-site reactions and occasional hypersensitivity being the main noted concerns. The larger caveat is the evidence gap: much of the supportive literature comes from the regions where it is marketed, and there is a shortage of large, independent, blinded trials to confirm the neurological benefits it's associated with.

It is not FDA approved, it is not a substitute for evidence-based stroke or brain-injury care, and purity varies among non-clinical suppliers. Anyone considering it — particularly after a serious neurological event — should do so only under a qualified healthcare provider. None of this is medical advice.

Cortexin FAQ

What is Cortexin?

Cortexin is a low-molecular-weight polypeptide complex derived from cerebral cortex tissue. It is used in Russian and some post-Soviet clinical practice as a neuroprotective agent, typically by intramuscular injection. It is not an FDA-approved medicine in the United States.

What is Cortexin used for?

In clinical practice where it is registered, it is referenced for neuroprotection and neurological recovery — including after stroke or traumatic brain injury, and for neuropathy and cognitive support. Outside those settings it is an educational reference only and not a treatment for any condition.

What is a common Cortexin dose?

A commonly referenced regimen is 10 mg once daily by intramuscular injection for about 10 days. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is Cortexin FDA approved?

No. Cortexin is registered and used in some countries but is not approved by the FDA, and the independent, high-quality evidence base is limited. It should not be treated as an established therapy outside medical supervision.
