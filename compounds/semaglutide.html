What is semaglutide?

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist — a medicine that mimics glucagon-like peptide-1, a hormone your gut releases after you eat. It was developed by Novo Nordisk and is one of the most widely prescribed metabolic drugs in the world, sold under three brand names: Ozempic and Rybelsus for type 2 diabetes, and Wegovy for chronic weight management.

Structurally it is a modified version of the natural GLP-1 peptide, engineered to resist the enzymes that normally break the hormone down within minutes. That modification is what gives semaglutide a long half-life and allows once-weekly dosing in its injectable forms, rather than the multiple-times-daily dosing that earlier GLP-1 drugs required.

Unlike most of the experimental peptides catalogued here, semaglutide is a fully approved drug backed by large randomized clinical trials. That matters: its benefits and risks are documented in regulatory labeling, and it is intended to be prescribed and monitored by a clinician rather than self-sourced.

How semaglutide is thought to work

GLP-1 is part of the body's natural "I've eaten" signaling system. By activating GLP-1 receptors, semaglutide reproduces and extends several of those signals at once:

  • Glucose-dependent insulin release — it prompts the pancreas to release more insulin when blood sugar is high, which is the basis of its use in type 2 diabetes.
  • Lower glucagon — it reduces secretion of glucagon, the hormone that tells the liver to pour out glucose, helping to steady blood-sugar levels.
  • Slower gastric emptying — it slows how quickly the stomach empties, so meals feel more filling and sustaining.
  • Appetite and reward signaling — it acts on appetite centers in the brain, reducing hunger and food cravings, which is the main driver of weight loss.

Together these effects explain why the same molecule helps both blood-sugar control and body weight: it tilts the whole system toward eating less and managing glucose more efficiently.

What the research shows

Semaglutide's evidence base is unusually strong for a compound in this catalog, because it is an approved medicine studied in large clinical-trial programs. The SUSTAIN trials established its glucose-lowering effect in type 2 diabetes, and the STEP program evaluated the higher-dose weight-management product, where participants achieved substantial average weight loss alongside lifestyle changes. A separate large cardiovascular-outcomes program (SELECT) reported benefit on cardiovascular events in people with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity without diabetes.

In other words, the approved uses rest on solid human data rather than anecdote. The honest caveats are that results vary between individuals, that weight tends to return if the medication is stopped without other changes, and that long-term use is still accumulating real-world safety experience. How semaglutide is commonly used

The defining feature of semaglutide dosing is slow titration. Approved regimens start at a low dose — commonly 0.25 mg weekly — and step up over a period of weeks toward a maintenance dose, up to 2.0 mg for the diabetes product or 2.4 mg for weight management. The gradual ramp exists specifically to let the gut adjust and to limit nausea; moving up too fast is the usual reason people feel unwell.

The approved pens are given as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection into the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm, on the same day each week. Rybelsus is an oral tablet version taken daily on an empty stomach. Commonly stacked with

Semaglutide is usually used on its own rather than "stacked." People researching it most often compare it with the other incretin-based metabolic compounds — tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1/GIP agonist, and retatrutide, an investigational triple agonist. Combining two GLP-1-class agents is not recommended and increases side-effect risk.

Side effects & safety considerations

The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, constipation, vomiting, and reflux — and they tend to be worst during dose increases, then ease as the body adapts. Less commonly, GLP-1 drugs have been associated with gallbladder problems and pancreatitis, and they can cause low blood sugar when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.

Semaglutide carries a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors observed in rodent studies, and it is contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). It is not recommended in pregnancy. Because it is a prescription medicine, these risks should be reviewed with a qualified healthcare provider, who can also screen for the conditions that make it inappropriate. Semaglutide FAQ

What is a common semaglutide dose?

Approved regimens start low — commonly 0.25 mg weekly — and are titrated upward over weeks toward a maintenance dose, up to 2.4 mg weekly for the weight-management product. The slow ramp is meant to limit nausea. Exact dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is semaglutide FDA approved?

Yes. Semaglutide is an approved prescription medicine, sold as Ozempic and Rybelsus for type 2 diabetes and as Wegovy for chronic weight management. It should be used under medical supervision.

How does semaglutide work for weight loss?

Semaglutide mimics the gut hormone GLP-1. It slows stomach emptying, signals fullness to the brain, and improves insulin response to meals — together these reduce appetite and overall food intake, which drives weight loss in the approved weight-management setting.

What is the difference between Ozempic and Wegovy?

Both are semaglutide. Ozempic is the brand approved for type 2 diabetes (max 2.0 mg weekly); Wegovy is the brand approved for chronic weight management and titrates to 2.4 mg weekly. Rybelsus is the oral tablet form of semaglutide.

What are the common side effects of semaglutide?

The most common effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, diarrhea, constipation, and reflux — and they tend to be worst during dose increases.
