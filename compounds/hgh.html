What is HGH?

HGH — human growth hormone, known as somatropin in its pharmaceutical form — is a peptide hormone produced by the pituitary gland. It is one of the body's central regulators of growth, repair, and metabolism: it drives growth in childhood and continues to influence body composition, tissue repair, and how the body handles fat and sugar throughout adult life.

Natural growth hormone is released in pulses, with the largest surges occurring during deep sleep. Recombinant HGH is a lab-made, identical copy of the human hormone, developed as a medicine to replace it when the body does not make enough. It is an FDA-approved drug for defined conditions — but it is also one of the most misused hormones in anti-aging and physique circles, which is where responsible framing matters most.

A key point of accuracy: using HGH for anti-aging or bodybuilding is not an approved use, and distribution for those purposes is specifically restricted under U.S. law. This page is educational and describes the hormone and its legitimate medical role.

How HGH is thought to work

Growth hormone acts in two ways — some effects are direct, but much of its signaling is mediated by a second hormone it triggers:

  • IGF-1 release — HGH prompts the liver (and other tissues) to produce insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), which carries out much of the tissue-growth and repair signaling attributed to growth hormone.
  • Lipolysis — it promotes the breakdown of stored fat for energy, one reason it is associated with changes in body composition.
  • Protein synthesis and tissue repair — it supports the building and maintenance of muscle, bone, and connective tissue.
  • Glucose handling — it can oppose insulin's effects, which is why higher doses can raise blood sugar and strain insulin sensitivity.

Because IGF-1 carries so much of the signal and is more stable in the blood than pulsatile growth hormone, clinicians typically monitor IGF-1 levels to judge whether dosing is appropriate.

What the research shows

In people with genuine growth hormone deficiency — children who don't produce enough and adults with deficiency from pituitary disease or injury — replacement therapy is well established and clearly beneficial, improving growth in children and body composition and quality of life in deficient adults. HGH also has several other specific approved indications. In these settings the evidence is solid.

The picture is very different for use in healthy adults seeking anti-aging or performance effects. Here the benefits are modest and uncertain, the risks are real, and major medical reviews have concluded it should not be used for anti-aging. It is also banned in competitive sport. The honest summary: valuable and evidence-based as replacement for a true deficiency, poorly justified and risky as a lifestyle drug. This page is not a substitute for a clinician's assessment.

How HGH is commonly used

Medical dosing for deficiency is individualized and usually low, started conservatively and titrated to IGF-1 bloodwork and response. Commonly referenced non-medical figures fall around 1-4 IU daily, typically taken in the morning. Timing is deliberate: because the body makes most of its own growth hormone during deep sleep, morning or pre-training dosing is often preferred, and taking HGH at bedtime alongside a secretagogue can blunt your natural nighttime pulse.

HGH is supplied as a delicate lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted gently with bacteriostatic water — it is sensitive to heat and rough handling — and kept refrigerated after mixing. It is dosed in international units, so getting the reconstitution concentration right is essential. Commonly compared with

People researching HGH often compare it with the growth hormone secretagogues — peptides like sermorelin, ipamorelin, and CJC-1295 that nudge the body to release more of its own growth hormone in a natural pulsatile pattern, rather than replacing it outright. Tesamorelin is a related releasing-factor analog. These work with the body's rhythm instead of overriding it.

Side effects & safety considerations

Common side effects include fluid retention, joint and muscle aches, carpal-tunnel-like symptoms (numbness and tingling in the hands), and insulin resistance that can raise blood sugar. These tend to be dose-related and more likely as doses climb above replacement levels. Because HGH influences cell growth, there are long-standing theoretical concerns about its interaction with existing tumors, and it is used cautiously in people with certain cancers or active retinopathy.

HGH is a prescription medicine, and non-medical use is both legally restricted and medically risky without monitoring of IGF-1 and glucose. Product quality from unregulated sources is unverified, which compounds the risk. None of this is medical advice: HGH should be prescribed, dosed, and monitored by a qualified healthcare provider.

HGH FAQ

What is a common HGH dose?

Medical growth-hormone-deficiency dosing is individualized and usually low, titrated to IGF-1 bloodwork. Commonly referenced non-medical figures fall around 1-4 IU daily, often taken in the morning. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

How do you reconstitute HGH?

HGH is supplied as a lyophilized powder and mixed gently with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection, then kept refrigerated. Because it is dosed in international units, StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator gives you the exact units to draw for your vial size and target dose.

Is HGH FDA approved?

Yes — as somatropin, for specific conditions such as growth hormone deficiency in children and adults and a handful of other approved indications. It is a prescription medicine, and using it for anti-aging or bodybuilding is not an approved use and is restricted by law.

Why is HGH often taken in the morning?

Timing is about not blunting your own rhythm. The body releases most of its natural growth hormone during deep sleep, so morning or pre-training dosing is often preferred, and taking HGH at bedtime alongside a secretagogue can interfere with that natural pulse. Timing should be set with a provider.

How does HGH actually work?

Growth hormone acts partly on its own and largely by prompting the liver to release IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1). IGF-1 mediates much of the tissue-growth and repair signaling, which is why clinicians monitor IGF-1 levels to gauge dosing.

What are the side effects of HGH?

Common effects include fluid retention, joint aches, carpal-tunnel-like symptoms, and insulin resistance that can raise blood sugar. At higher doses risks increase. It should be supervised by a provider, with IGF-1 and glucose monitored.
