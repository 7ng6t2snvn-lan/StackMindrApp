What is Melanotan I?

Melanotan I is a synthetic analog of alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (alpha-MSH), a natural hormone that tells pigment cells in the skin to produce melanin. By mimicking alpha-MSH, Melanotan I can increase skin pigmentation — a tan — with less ultraviolet exposure than would normally be required. It is a modified, more stable version of the natural hormone.

Here is what sets it apart from most compounds in this glossary: the exact same molecule, under the name afamelanotide, is an FDA-approved prescription medicine. Marketed as Scenesse, it is delivered as a small implant to help patients with erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP), a rare genetic disorder that makes sunlight extremely painful. That approval is specific and narrow — it does not make the injectable powder sold online for cosmetic tanning an approved product.

Melanotan I is often grouped with its cousin Melanotan II, but they differ: Melanotan I is more selective for pigmentation and is generally described as having fewer of the libido and other systemic effects associated with Melanotan II.

How Melanotan I is thought to work

Its mechanism follows directly from mimicking alpha-MSH:

  • Activating melanocortin-1 receptors (MC1R) — on the pigment-producing cells (melanocytes) in the skin.
  • Stimulating melanin production — specifically eumelanin, the darker pigment, which deepens skin color and provides some photoprotection.
  • Greater receptor selectivity than Melanotan II — which is why Melanotan I is associated mainly with pigmentation and fewer of the sexual or appetite effects tied to broader melanocortin activity.

In EPP, the medical rationale is that increasing eumelanin raises the skin's tolerance to light, reducing the severe pain patients experience with sun exposure — which is the basis of the approved afamelanotide implant.

What the research shows

Melanotan I / afamelanotide has a stronger evidence base than most peptides here, precisely because it went through formal drug development. Clinical trials in EPP demonstrated that afamelanotide increased pain-free light exposure, which supported its approval as Scenesse for that specific condition in both the US and Europe.

For cosmetic tanning, the pigment-stimulating effect is real and well understood mechanistically, but the injectable powder people buy for that purpose is unregulated and unstudied as a product. So the accurate picture is: solid clinical evidence for the approved implant in EPP, but the DIY tanning use sits entirely outside that approved, studied context.

How Melanotan I is commonly used

In cosmetic use, commonly referenced protocols involve a loading phase followed by less frequent maintenance, with per-dose figures often around 0.5–1 mg, given every other day or a couple of times weekly by subcutaneous injection. The approved medical product, by contrast, is a controlled-release implant placed by a clinician — a very different delivery method from the injectable powder.

The injectable version is supplied as a lyophilized powder that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Commonly stacked with

Melanotan I is most naturally compared with Melanotan II, its broader-acting cousin that also affects libido and appetite, and with PT-141 (bremelanotide), a related melanocortin peptide developed specifically for sexual function. These share the melanocortin-system mechanism but are used for quite different goals.

Side effects & safety considerations

Commonly reported effects include nausea (especially after dosing), flushing, and darkening of existing moles and freckles. That last point is the most important safety consideration: because Melanotan I changes pigmentation, it can alter moles, which can complicate skin-cancer monitoring. Anyone using it — or considering it — should keep up regular skin checks, and new or changing moles warrant prompt dermatological review.

While afamelanotide is approved and studied for EPP, the injectable powder sold for tanning is unregulated, varies in purity, and does not have a consumer safety record. None of this is medical advice. Anyone considering Melanotan I should discuss it with a qualified healthcare provider, and EPP is a medical condition that requires proper care.

Melanotan I FAQ

What does Melanotan I do?

It mimics alpha-MSH and activates MC1R on skin pigment cells, stimulating melanin production. The result is increased skin pigmentation (a tan) with less UV exposure than normally required.

Is Melanotan I the same as afamelanotide?

Yes. Afamelanotide is the same molecule, and as a controlled-release implant called Scenesse it is FDA-approved for the rare light-sensitivity disorder EPP. The injectable powder sold for tanning is not an approved product.

How is Melanotan I different from Melanotan II?

Melanotan I is more selective for pigmentation and is generally described as having fewer libido and appetite effects, while Melanotan II acts more broadly on the melanocortin system and is associated with those additional effects.

What is a common Melanotan I dose?

Cosmetic protocols often reference around 0.5–1 mg every other day or a couple of times weekly by subcutaneous injection. This is educational reference only — dosing should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Does Melanotan I affect moles?

Yes. It can darken existing moles and freckles, which can complicate skin-cancer monitoring. Regular skin checks are important, and any new or changing mole should be reviewed by a dermatologist.
