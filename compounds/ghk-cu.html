What is GHK-Cu?

GHK-Cu is a copper peptide: the tripeptide glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine (GHK) bound to a copper ion. Unlike most of the peptides discussed in this space, it is not synthetic in origin — GHK occurs naturally in human plasma, saliva, and urine, and it has a strong affinity for copper, forming the stable GHK-Cu complex. Its characteristic deep-blue color in solution comes directly from that copper content.

One of the most cited facts about GHK is that its level in the body declines with age — roughly falling from youth into older adulthood — and researchers have long proposed that this decline parallels the body's diminishing capacity to repair tissue. That observation is a large part of why GHK-Cu became interesting as a "regenerative" and anti-aging molecule.

Practically, GHK-Cu lives a double life. It is a well-established cosmetic ingredient found in serums and creams for skin, and it is also used as a reconstituted injectable in peptide protocols aimed at more systemic repair, healing, and hair support. Its strongest, best-documented role is the topical, skin-focused one.

How GHK-Cu is thought to work

GHK-Cu is unusual because it appears to act broadly rather than through a single receptor. The actions most often described in the research literature include:

  • Copper delivery — it carries copper, a cofactor for enzymes involved in collagen and elastin cross-linking and in antioxidant defense, into cells.
  • Collagen and matrix stimulation — it is reported to stimulate fibroblasts to produce collagen, elastin, and other extracellular-matrix components that give skin its firmness and structure.
  • Wound-healing signaling — it is associated with attracting repair cells, supporting new blood-vessel formation (angiogenesis), and remodeling of damaged tissue.
  • Antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity — it has been shown in research models to reduce oxidative and inflammatory signaling.
  • Gene expression — notably, studies have reported that GHK can influence the activity of a large number of human genes, shifting expression toward a more "repair" and regenerative pattern.

This wide-ranging profile is why GHK-Cu is talked about for skin, hair, and healing all at once, rather than for one narrow effect.

What the research shows

GHK-Cu has a substantial research base for a peptide, especially in dermatology and cosmetic science. Laboratory and skin studies support its role in collagen production, skin firmness and texture, and wound repair, and it is a long-accepted cosmetic active with meaningful topical evidence. There is also preclinical work on hair-follicle support and broad "regenerative" gene-expression effects, much of it associated with researcher Loren Pickart, who first isolated GHK decades ago.

Where to stay measured: the strongest, most repeatable evidence is topical and cosmetic. The injectable, systemic uses — for internal healing, recovery, or anti-aging throughout the body — rest on far less human data, and the dramatic whole-body claims are largely extrapolated from lab and animal work. The honest summary is "well-supported as a skin active, much less proven as a systemic injectable."

How GHK-Cu is commonly used

GHK-Cu is used two different ways:

  • Topical — in serums and creams at low concentrations, applied to the skin for firmness, texture, and repair. This is its most established and lowest-risk use.
  • Injectable — reconstituted and given subcutaneously, with commonly referenced protocols around 1-2 mg daily, sometimes run in multi-week cycles, for people pursuing systemic repair or hair goals.

Injectable GHK-Cu arrives as a lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder — distinctly blue because of its copper — that must be reconstituted with bacteriostatic water before use. Side effects & safety considerations

Topical GHK-Cu is generally well tolerated; the most commonly reported issues are mild skin irritation, redness, or itching, and occasional sensitivity when combined with strong actives like high-strength vitamin C. As an injectable, the most commonly mentioned effects are injection-site reactions.

The specific consideration with GHK-Cu is the copper. Copper is an essential trace mineral, but it is also possible to take in too much, and the long-term safety of repeated systemic copper-peptide injection is simply not well characterized. People with Wilson's disease or other disorders of copper handling should be especially cautious, and anyone considering injectable use should be mindful of cumulative copper exposure. Product purity varies across suppliers of an unregulated injectable. None of this is medical advice — anyone considering GHK-Cu, particularly by injection, should review it with a qualified healthcare provider who knows their history.

GHK-Cu FAQ

What is a common GHK-Cu dose?

For injectable use, commonly referenced protocols fall around 1-2 mg daily. In skincare it is used topically at low concentrations. This is educational reference only — any dose should be set with a qualified healthcare provider.

Is GHK-Cu used topically or by injection?

Both. GHK-Cu is a long-established cosmetic ingredient in topical serums and creams for skin, and it is also used as a reconstituted subcutaneous injection in peptide protocols aimed at systemic repair and healing.

What is GHK-Cu good for?

It is best known in connection with collagen and skin — it is studied for skin repair, firmness, wound healing, and hair follicle support, and has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity in research models. Benefits are most established topically; systemic injectable claims are less proven.

How do you reconstitute GHK-Cu?

Injectable GHK-Cu is supplied as a lyophilized powder (distinctly blue from its copper content) and mixed with bacteriostatic water before subcutaneous injection. StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator gives you the exact units to draw for your vial size and target dose.

Is GHK-Cu FDA approved?

GHK-Cu is a widely used cosmetic ingredient, not an FDA-approved drug. As an injectable it is a research compound and is not a treatment for any condition. Discuss any use with a qualified healthcare provider.
