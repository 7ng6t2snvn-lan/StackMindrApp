How the reconstitution math works

Concentration is how much compound ends up in each millilitre of solution: the amount in the vial divided by the BAC (bacteriostatic) water you add. More water means a weaker mix and a bigger, easier-to-read draw; less water means a stronger mix and a smaller draw.

Units to draw is the number that matters at the needle. Insulin syringes are U-100, meaning 100 units per millilitre — so once you know the volume for your dose, the unit mark is just that volume × 100. A 1cc, 0.5cc, and 0.3cc syringe all use the same scale; they only differ in how much they hold.

Doses per vial tells you how long a vial lasts at your dose, so you can reorder before you run dry. For HGH the calculator uses the standard 1 mg = 3 IU conversion; for HCG and peptides it works directly from the IU or mg you enter.

Estimated blood levels is a separate mode. Enter a compound's half-life, your dose, and how often you take it, and the chart shows how the amount in your system rises with each dose and flattens out at steady state — along with how many days and doses that takes and your peak-to-trough swing. You can overlay a second compound to compare how fast each one builds and how steady it stays. It's a single-compartment half-life estimate for education, not a blood test. Reconstitution calculator FAQ

How do you calculate peptide reconstitution?

Two steps. First, concentration = the amount in the vial ÷ the BAC water you add (strength per mL). Then, units to draw = (your dose ÷ concentration) × 100, since insulin syringes are U-100. This calculator runs both steps and shows the concentration, volume, units, and doses per vial once you enter your numbers.

How much BAC water should I mix with a peptide vial?

There's no single right amount — it's a choice that sets how concentrated the mix is. More water = a weaker solution and a larger, easier-to-read draw; less water = a stronger solution and a smaller draw. Common choices are 1, 2, or 3 mL. Enter what you plan to use and the calculator shows the resulting concentration. Confirm your approach with a qualified provider.

How do I know how many units to draw on an insulin syringe?

Insulin syringes are U-100 (100 units per mL), so the unit mark = injection volume in mL × 100. Once the calculator knows your concentration and dose, it works out the volume and converts it to units for you. A 1cc, 0.5cc, and 0.3cc syringe all use the same scale.

How do I calculate a semaglutide or tirzepatide dose?

GLP-1 compounds reconstitute like any other peptide: concentration = mg in the vial ÷ BAC water, and units to draw = dose volume × 100. Enter vial and dose in matching units. This is educational reference only — your dose should be set by your prescriber. See the semaglutide and tirzepatide pages for more.

Can this calculator estimate blood levels or steady state?

Yes — the Estimated levels tab charts how a compound accumulates with repeated dosing and roughly when it plateaus at steady state, from its half-life, your dose, and your interval. It shows the days to about 90% of steady state, how many doses that takes, and your peak-to-trough swing, and you can overlay a second compound to compare how fast each one builds and how steady it stays. It's a single-compartment half-life estimate for education — not a blood test or medical advice.

How do you reconstitute HCG?

HCG is a lyophilized powder measured in international units (IU), mixed with BAC or sterile water before subcutaneous injection. Enter the IU in the vial and the water you add, and the calculator gives the concentration and units to draw for your target IU dose.

How do you convert HGH from mg to IU?

The standard conversion is 1 mg = 3 IU. The HGH tab applies it automatically, so you can enter your vial and dose in whichever unit your product is labeled with and still get the correct units to draw.

Does the syringe size change how many units I draw?

No. The units for a given dose are the same on a 1cc, 0.5cc, or 0.3cc syringe — all are U-100. They only differ in capacity: a 0.3cc holds at most 30 units, so if a dose needs more volume than your syringe holds you'd split it or size up. The calculator flags this automatically.

Is this reconstitution calculator accurate?

It uses the same standard concentration and U-100 unit math as the StackMindr app, and shows its work so you can verify every figure. It's a mathematical tool for educational purposes, not medical advice — always double-check your own numbers and work with a qualified healthcare provider.
