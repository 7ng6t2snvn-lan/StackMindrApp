Peptide Bioregulators

The short "bioregulator" peptides tied to the research of Prof. Vladimir Khavinson — each proposed to act as a tissue-specific switch for one organ. This guide explains the hypothesis plainly, is honest about how thin the independent evidence is, and lists the 40 you'll see named most often.

What are peptide bioregulators?

Peptide bioregulators are very short peptides — often just two to four amino acids — associated with the research of Prof. Vladimir Khavinson and the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology. Over several decades that group catalogued dozens of them, each tied to a specific organ: one for the thymus, one for the pineal gland, one for the liver, one for the retina, and so on.

The central hypothesis is that each short peptide acts as a tissue-specific regulator of gene expression and protein synthesis. The idea is that the peptide travels to its target organ, interacts with DNA, and nudges aged or stressed tissue back toward more youthful patterns of protein production. It's an elegant theory — but it is a theory, and the detailed molecular mechanism is still debated.

They're usually sold in two forms:

  • Natural tissue-extract peptides — peptide complexes isolated from animal organ tissue, typically given by injection. These are the original "Cytomax"-style preparations.
  • Synthetic short peptides — lab-made di-, tri-, or tetrapeptides built to copy the active fragment, usually taken orally in capsules. These are the "Cytogen"-style versions.

One honest caveat up front: most of the published research comes from a single research group, independent human replication is limited, and none of these are approved medicines in the United States or most of the world. Treat everything below as an experimental research reference, not a proven therapy.

How they're commonly used & stacked

The pattern you'll see described most often is short cycles of roughly 10 to 20 days, repeated a few times a year rather than taken continuously — the stated rationale being that a brief "regulatory signal" is enough and that tissue doesn't need a constant supply. Synthetic versions are usually oral capsules taken on an empty stomach; the natural tissue-extract versions are usually injected subcutaneously or intramuscularly. If you're reconstituting an injectable, StackMindr's free reconstitution calculator handles the draw-volume math.

Because each peptide targets a different organ, people often combine several — for example a thymus peptide with the pineal peptide, or an organ-specific peptide paired with epitalon as a general "base." The most common longevity pairing you'll encounter is endoluten or epitalon with a thymus peptide such as thymalin or vladonix. None of this is a dosing recommendation, and the combinations have not been validated in controlled human trials — any protocol should be set with a knowledgeable provider.

Peptide Bioregulators FAQ

What are peptide bioregulators?

They come in two forms: natural peptide complexes isolated from animal organ tissue (usually injectable) and synthetic short peptides designed to copy the active fragment (usually taken orally). Each is linked to a specific organ or tissue, such as the brain, thymus, liver, or pineal gland.

How are peptide bioregulators supposed to work?

The hypothesis is that each short peptide acts as a tissue-specific regulator — binding to DNA and influencing gene expression and protein synthesis in one particular organ, nudging aged or stressed tissue back toward normal function. This is a hypothesis, not settled science. It is the framework proposed by the research group that developed these peptides, and the detailed molecular mechanism is still debated.

Is the research on bioregulators solid?

It should be read with caution. Most of the published work comes from a single research group, independent replication in humans is limited, and much of the evidence is preclinical or from small studies.
